# Gas Pipeline Monitoring: Companies, Systems, Technologies, Challenges, and Solutions

*Prepared July 2026*

---

## 1. Introduction

Gas pipeline networks move natural gas, hydrogen blends, CO₂, and other products across hundreds of thousands of miles of onshore and offshore infrastructure. Monitoring this infrastructure is a layered discipline combining control-room software (SCADA/leak detection), physical sensing (fiber optics, acoustic, IoT), in-line inspection ("smart pigging"), aerial/satellite surveillance, and cybersecurity. No single technology covers every failure mode, so operators typically deploy several overlapping systems.

---

## 2. Core Technology Categories

### 2.1 SCADA and Computational Pipeline Monitoring (CPM) Software
The control-room backbone. SCADA systems collect pressure, temperature, and flow data from field instrumentation and feed it into leak-detection algorithms — most commonly **Real-Time Transient Modeling (RTTM)**, mass/volume balance, and statistical/negative-pressure-wave methods, per the API RP 1130 standard.

- **Emerson** — PipelineManager Software Suite (RTTM, Analytical Leak Detection, Rupture Detection, VisualPipeline GUI), integrates with SCADA/DCS/RTU/PLC systems.
- **Honeywell** — Experion PKS platform with Experion Leak Detection, combining hydraulic modeling with live operational data and integrated alarm/fire-and-gas/emergency shutdown systems.
- **Siemens** — pipeline monitoring integration across digital surveillance and IoT-enabled control platforms for midstream operators.
- **ABB** and **Schneider Electric** — round out the "big five" that collectively hold roughly 40% of the pipeline leak-detection software market.
- **Atmos International** — specialist vendor focused purely on leak detection, theft detection, and simulation; often paired with integrators (e.g., EnerSys) to reduce false alarms and support NTSB recommendations for improved leak mitigation.
- **PSI Software**, **Vanmok** (SCADA-independent, pressure-transmitter-based rupture detection compliant with PHMSA's Final Rule), and various niche RTTM vendors compete in this space.

### 2.2 Distributed Fiber Optic Sensing (DFOS)
A fiber-optic cable laid along or near the pipe becomes a continuous sensor. Three main modes are used, often combined:
- **DAS (Distributed Acoustic Sensing)** — detects vibration signatures from leaks, digging, tapping, or vehicle/foot traffic near the line.
- **DTS (Distributed Temperature Sensing)** — detects temperature anomalies from escaping gas (Joule-Thomson cooling) or ground disturbance.
- **DSS/DTSS (Distributed Strain Sensing)** — detects pipe bending, ground movement, and geohazard-induced stress.

Key vendors: **OptaSense** (Interrogation Units covering ~50 miles per site, aggregating to monitor thousands of miles centrally), **AP Sensing** (DAS/DTS/DTSS fusion, PIG-tracking, intrusion detection), **Bandweaver**, **FEBUS Optics**, **Hawk Measurement Systems** (Praetorian system), and **DALI** (utility-focused, 5-meter leak/intrusion accuracy). **DNV** acts as an independent verifier/certifier of DFOS system performance against international standards.

### 2.3 In-Line Inspection ("Smart Pigging")
Instrumented tools travel inside the pipe to detect corrosion, cracks, dents, and metal loss using magnetic flux leakage (MFL), ultrasonic testing (UT), and electromagnetic acoustic transducers (EMAT).

- **ROSEN Group** (Germany) — over 40 years in the field, has inspected over a million miles of pipeline; heavy investment in autonomous inspection robotics (~$75M, 2025) and data analytics platforms.
- **Baker Hughes** (via its legacy Pipeline Inspection (PII) and Process & Pipeline Services divisions) — advanced NDT/sensor technology; acquired Pipeline Analytics Corporation (2025, ~$180M) to deepen machine-learning-based digital twin capability.
- **T.D. Williamson (TDW)** — century-old ILI and hot-tapping/plugging specialist; launched "SmartPig 4.0" combining EMAT and ultrasonic probes.
- **NDT Global** — ultrasonic/EMAT specialist; secured a five-year, ~$120M integrity contract covering 4,800 km of Enbridge's Canadian Mainline.
- Other notable players: **Quest Integrity Group**, **Halliburton**, **Applus+**, **Enduro Pipeline Services**, **Onstream Pipeline Inspection**, **Intertek**, **Cokebusters**, **A.Hak Industrial Services**, **Innospection**, **MISTRAS Group**.

### 2.4 Satellite and Aerial Monitoring
Used mainly for methane emissions detection, right-of-way surveillance, and geohazard mapping.

- **GHGSat** — largest commercial methane-monitoring constellation (16 satellites as of late 2025, targeting further expansion), detection threshold as low as ~100 kg/hr, facility-level attribution; major clients include ExxonMobil, Chevron, Aramco, and government agencies.
- **Kayrros** — AI-driven analytics layered on data from Sentinel-5P (TROPOMI), GHGSat, and commercial radar satellites for near-real-time global methane alerts.
- **MethaneSAT** (Environmental Defense Fund) — area-flux measurement satellite launched March 2024; notably, contact with the satellite was lost in mid-2025, illustrating the operational fragility of dedicated single-satellite missions. Its dataset remains available via Google Earth Engine.
- **Carbon Mapper** (Tanager satellite) and NOAA's **GOES** weather satellites also contribute point-source and large-emission-event detection.
- **Satelytics** and **Rezatec** — commercial platforms that fuse satellite, drone, aircraft, and fixed-camera imagery with proprietary analytics for leak/vegetation/encroachment alerts (clients include Chevron, Duke Energy).
- Drone (UAV)-based inspection is increasingly used for right-of-way and above-ground facility surveillance, often paired with infrared/optical gas imaging cameras.

### 2.5 IoT Sensor Networks and Wireless Telemetry
Because pipelines often run through remote, low-connectivity terrain, operators increasingly deploy low-power wide-area networks — **LoRaWAN**, **NB-IoT**, and **Zigbee** mesh topologies — to relay pressure, temperature, and flow data from field sensors back to SCADA in near real time without requiring extensive fixed communication infrastructure.

### 2.6 Acoustic, Infrared, and Point Sensors
- Non-intrusive acoustic monitoring detects debris, cavitation, and seal degradation in valves and pumps.
- Infrared/optical gas imaging cameras (fixed or drone-mounted) visualize gas plumes invisible to the naked eye.
- Traditional point sensors (catalytic, infrared, semiconductor) remain common at compressor stations and metering facilities but require dense deployment for adequate coverage and are sensitive to airflow, humidity, and placement.

## 3. Problems and Challenges

| Challenge | Description |
|---|---|
| **Aging infrastructure** | Large portions of legacy pipeline networks predate modern monitoring standards; PHMSA tracks the phased removal of aging, higher-risk pipe (e.g., cast iron, bare steel) as part of its safety data program. |
| **False alarms / detection accuracy** | Sensor noise, environmental vibration, and threshold-based systems generate false positives, which erode operator trust and can lead to "alarm fatigue," while overly conservative thresholds risk missed small leaks. |
| **Interoperability** | Integrating monitoring software from different vendors with legacy SCADA/DCS systems creates friction, often requiring costly custom integration and extending project timelines. |
| **Coverage in remote terrain** | Long pipeline routes cross areas with limited cellular or fixed communications, complicating continuous real-time monitoring. |
| **Cybersecurity** | The 2021 Colonial Pipeline ransomware attack exposed vulnerabilities in industrial control systems; PHMSA, TSA, CISA, and DOE now coordinate on control-room cybersecurity inspections, and threats to SCADA/ICS environments remain a top concern. |
| **Cost of sensor infrastructure** | High installation costs for physical sensing infrastructure (fiber runs, DFOS interrogators) remain a barrier, especially for smaller and developing-market operators. |
| **Third-party interference/damage** | Excavation, construction, and illegal tapping cause a large share of incidents; passive alarm and encroachment-detection methods have historically been slow, expensive, or prone to false positives. |
| **Data localization/regulatory fragmentation** | Some jurisdictions (e.g., Russia, China, parts of the Middle East) mandate that monitoring data stay within national borders, limiting cloud-based deployment options. |
| **Satellite mission reliability** | Even well-funded methane-monitoring missions can fail unexpectedly — MethaneSAT lost contact with ground control in 2025 — underscoring the need for redundant, multi-source monitoring architectures. |
| **Emissions undercounting** | Independent satellite data has repeatedly shown that national inventories and self-reported figures understate methane emissions from oil & gas and landfill sources by wide margins. |
| **Workforce and inspection logistics** | Manual visual inspection is limited by human error, intermittent coverage, and the difficulty of accessing remote or hazardous terrain. |

## 4. Benefits of Modern Monitoring Systems

- **Faster leak detection and localization**, often to within a few meters, reducing environmental and safety impact and repair costs.
- **Predictive/preventive maintenance** — combining ILI data, corrosion modeling (Bayesian growth prediction), and machine learning to schedule repairs before failures occur rather than reacting after them.
- **Regulatory compliance** — automated data logging and reporting support compliance with PHMSA, API RP 1130, and emerging methane-reporting frameworks (e.g., OGMP 2.0, now covering roughly 40% of global oil & gas production).
- **Reduced non-revenue losses** — DFOS-based systems are frequently marketed as costing under 1% of full pipeline replacement while extending asset life.
- **Third-party intrusion prevention** — real-time detection of digging, tapping, or vehicle activity near the right-of-way enables intervention before damage occurs.
- **Unified situational awareness** — integration of leak detection, fire & gas systems, and emergency shutdown into a single SCADA-based platform.
- **Independent verification of emissions and leaks** — satellite and third-party analytics provide a check on operator self-reporting, increasingly used by regulators, financial institutions (e.g., Norges Bank, AP7), and insurers in engagement/divestment decisions.

## 5. Solutions and Emerging Trends

1. **Sensor fusion** — combining DAS, DTS, and DSS (or acoustic, pressure-wave, and RTTM methods) into a single system reduces false alarms and improves the reliability required to distinguish genuine leaks from background noise.
2. **AI/machine learning analytics** — increasingly applied to ILI data (digital twins), SCADA-based leak detection (e.g., new ML platforms analyzing standard SCADA data without new hardware), and satellite plume attribution, improving both sensitivity and reduction of false positives.
3. **Multi-layered emissions monitoring architecture** — layering satellite (wide-area, e.g., ESA's Copernicus CO2M), aircraft, drone, and ground-based sensors provides redundancy against single-mission failures like MethaneSAT's 2025 loss of contact.
4. **Autonomous inspection robotics** — investment (e.g., ROSEN's robotics program) in robots for hazardous or hard-to-pig pipeline segments, and human-robot co-dispatch planning for site surveillance.
5. **Low-power wireless mesh networks** (LoRaWAN, NB-IoT, Zigbee) to extend monitoring into remote areas without heavy fixed infrastructure investment.
6. **Cybersecurity-by-design** — PHMSA, TSA, and CISA are pushing operators toward built-in ICS/SCADA security, joint cybersecurity exercises, and cyber-risk considerations embedded in emergency response and integrity management plans.
7. **Standardized performance verification** — independent third parties (e.g., DNV) certifying DFOS and other systems against international standards to build operator and regulator confidence.
8. **Consolidation and M&A** — large players (Baker Hughes, Emerson, Siemens) are acquiring specialist analytics firms to combine hardware, ILI, and AI-driven software into end-to-end integrity platforms.

## 7. Case Study: Ghana National Gas Company (GNGC / "Ghana Gas")

Ghana's midstream gas sector — anchored by the state-owned Ghana National Gas Company (GNGC), the Atuabo processing plant, and the regional West African Gas Pipeline (WAGP) — faces its own version of the global challenges above, plus several that are more acute in a developing, import-dependent, cross-border network.

### 7.1 Ghana-specific challenges

| Challenge | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Gas theft via smuggling** | Ghana loses an estimated $200 million a year to petroleum-product smuggling, including subsidized fuels and gas products moved across porous land borders for resale, according to National Petroleum Authority officials. This directly parallels the "third-party interference" problem seen globally, but with an organized commercial-fraud dimension rather than just accidental damage. |
| **Siphoning** | Regulators have flagged siphoning of LPG from bulk delivery vehicles into unauthorized surface tanks before product reaches its declared destination — a supply-chain leakage point distinct from a physical pipeline leak, but one that similarly erodes volumetric accountability and revenue. Illegal tapping/hot-tapping of pipeline infrastructure to divert product (as documented extensively on Nigeria's Niger Delta pipelines that feed the WAGP corridor Ghana relies on) is a related regional risk. |
| **Dependence on a single cross-border import pipeline** | Ghana draws a significant share of its gas from the WAGP, which originates in Nigeria and passes through Benin and Togo before reaching Takoradi/Tema. Upstream maintenance shutdowns or supply disruptions in Nigeria have caused blackouts in Ghana, Togo, and Benin, and WAGP has a documented history of leaks and delivery interruptions since commissioning. |
| **Payment and commercial security across the regional value chain** | Industry figures have publicly flagged recurring payment and supply disputes along the WAGP system as a barrier to regional integration — a "soft" integrity issue (commercial/contractual) layered on top of the physical monitoring challenge. |
| **Growing demand outpacing domestic supply** | GNPC projects Ghana's gas demand could reach ~840 MMscfd by 2030 (and approach 1 Bcfd longer-term) while domestic production from maturing fields like Jubilee and TEN naturally declines, pushing Ghana toward LNG imports and tighter reliance on imported/transit infrastructure that is harder to directly monitor and secure. |
| **Financing and project delays** | Expansion projects (e.g., the second gas-processing train, the Wales gas-to-energy project) have faced multi-year delays tied to financing gaps, including disbursement issues on major development-bank facilities — this constrains investment in modern monitoring/SCADA upgrades alongside capacity expansion. |
| **Aging and mixed-vintage infrastructure** | The Atuabo plant and connecting pipelines have been operating for roughly a decade-plus, with newer assets (AKSA pipeline acquisition, TDS HIPPS) being added onto older infrastructure — creating the same "legacy + new" interoperability challenge described in Section 3, but with less mature domestic technical capacity to manage it. |
| **Remote and offshore exposure** | Offshore fields (Jubilee, TEN) and the subsea/coastal WAGP corridor are harder to visually inspect and more exposed to third-party interference than a fully onshore, urban network. |
| **Limited domestic monitoring/cybersecurity capacity** | As a relatively young midstream operator, GNGC has less in-house legacy investment in advanced DFOS, satellite, or ML-based leak analytics compared to established Gulf, European, or North American operators — meaning much of this capability would need to be sourced from the international vendors covered in Sections 2 and 6. |

### 7.2 How the technologies and solutions from this report apply

| Ghana Gas challenge | Relevant solution(s) from Sections 2–5 |
|---|---|
| **Smuggling of gas/LPG products** | SCADA-based mass/volume balance and RTTM software (Emerson, Honeywell, Atmos International) can flag unexplained volume discrepancies between metered input and delivered output — the same principle used to catch pipeline leaks can catch diversion for resale. Pairing this with **IoT-based volumetric metering** at custody-transfer and distribution points closes the accountability gap that enables smuggling. |
| **Siphoning from bulk vehicles/surface tanks** | This occurs downstream of the pipeline, so the fix is less about pipeline sensing and more about **chain-of-custody monitoring**: tamper-evident IoT-connected flow meters and GPS/telemetry on bulk trucks (an extension of the LoRaWAN/NB-IoT mesh approach in Section 2.5), combined with automated reconciliation in SCADA/ERP systems to flag volume loss between loading and delivery points. |
| **Illegal tapping / third-party interference along transit corridors** | **Distributed Acoustic and Temperature Sensing (DAS/DTS)** (OptaSense, AP Sensing, Bandweaver, FEBUS Optics) can detect the vibration and thermal signatures of hot-tapping or unauthorized excavation in real time and pinpoint the location to within meters — directly addressing the same "third-party intrusion" problem documented on the Nigerian pipelines feeding WAGP. |
| **Dependence on a single cross-border pipeline / upstream disruptions** | **Satellite and aerial monitoring** (GHGSat, Kayrros, Satelytics) plus **RTTM-based leak/flow modeling** give Ghana and WAGP operators independent, real-time visibility into supply status across the full multi-country corridor, rather than relying solely on the upstream operator's own reporting — improving early warning of disruptions and leaks anywhere along the 678 km line. |
| **Payment/commercial security disputes** | Not a sensing problem, but the **data transparency** produced by shared, independently verified (e.g., DNV-style) monitoring systems can reduce disputes over actual delivered volumes between GNGC, WAPCo, and neighboring off-takers — turning a trust problem into a measurable one. |
| **Growing demand / reliance on LNG imports** | Better monitoring supports higher **utilization confidence** — WAGP currently runs at roughly 60% peak utilization, and reliable, verifiable throughput data makes it easier for GNGC and regional partners to justify unlocking that spare capacity rather than over-investing in new import infrastructure. |
| **Financing and project delays** | Modern **SCADA-independent, pressure-transmitter-based rupture detection** (e.g., Vanmok's approach) offers a lower capital-cost entry point for leak/intrusion detection compared to full DFOS builds — useful for a gas company balancing expansion capex against monitoring capex. |
| **Aging/mixed-vintage infrastructure** | The same **interoperability-focused integration work** flagged in Section 3 (custom middleware linking legacy SCADA to newer leak-detection and analytics layers) applies directly as GNGC brings the AKSA pipeline and second processing train online alongside older Atuabo-era assets. |
| **Offshore/remote exposure** | **Drone and satellite-based right-of-way surveillance** plus **low-power wireless telemetry** extend monitoring coverage to offshore platforms and coastal corridors without requiring dense fixed communications infrastructure. |
| **Limited domestic monitoring capacity** | GNGC could pursue the same **vendor partnership / systems-integrator model** used elsewhere in the industry (e.g., EnerSys–Atmos International pairing) — importing proven leak-detection and DFOS expertise while building internal capacity, rather than developing monitoring technology from scratch. |

### 7.3 Takeaway

Ghana Gas's core vulnerability isn't just "leaks" in the classic sense — it's **volumetric accountability across a multi-party, cross-border, mixed-legacy system**, where product can be lost to physical leaks, illegal tapping, smuggling, or siphoning at multiple points between the wellhead and the end customer. The technologies in this report that add the most value for Ghana specifically are: (1) fiber-optic/acoustic sensing for physical tampering and leaks along the pipeline right-of-way, (2) SCADA/RTTM-based volume reconciliation to catch diversion (smuggling/siphoning) rather than just structural leaks, and (3) independent satellite verification to give Ghana visibility into a pipeline system it doesn't fully control end-to-end.


OSINT is a rich discipline — here's a structured breakdown of how it works, from raw sources to actionable intelligence.

## The Core Idea

OSINT treats publicly accessible information as raw material. The value isn't in any single data point — it's in **correlation**: connecting scattered, individually innocuous pieces into a picture that none of them reveal alone.

## Source Categories

**Direct sources** — information the target (person, org, system) deliberately or knowingly puts out:

- Social media posts, bios, connections
- Company websites, press releases, job postings
- Public filings (SEC, Companies House, court records)
- Published papers, patents, conference talks
- Domain/WHOIS registrations, SSL certificates

**Indirect sources** — information exposed as a byproduct, not intentionally:

- Metadata in files/images (EXIF data, document authorship, timestamps)
- Cached or archived pages (Wayback Machine) showing what changed and when
- DNS records, subdomain enumeration, exposed cloud storage buckets
- Data breach dumps (for defensive/verification purposes)
- Third-party mentions — tagged photos, forum replies, review sites, sublet listings
- Code repositories (commit history, leaked credentials, comments revealing infrastructure)
- Satellite/aerial imagery, geolocation clues in photos (shadows, signage, architecture)

## The Inference Layer (where OSINT becomes "intelligent")

This is the actual skill — turning fragments into conclusions:

1. **Cross-referencing** — matching a username across platforms, or a phone number in a leaked breach against a LinkedIn profile, to link identities.
2. **Temporal analysis** — posting patterns revealing time zones, work schedules, or travel (e.g., a sudden gap in social activity correlating with a business trip mentioned elsewhere).
3. **Pattern-of-life** — combining gym check-ins, commute photos, and routine posts to infer a person's habits or location patterns.
4. **Organizational mapping** — job postings revealing tech stack ("looking for a Kubernetes engineer") which infers internal infrastructure; LinkedIn org charts inferring reporting structures even without an official chart.
5. **Negative space reasoning** — noticing what's *missing* (a deleted tweet via cache, a scrubbed employee bio) can be as telling as what's present.
6. **Geolocation triangulation** — matching background details in one photo against street-view imagery or other geotagged photos from the same area.
7. **Linguistic fingerprinting** — writing style, phrasing, or even typo patterns linking pseudonymous accounts to a known identity.

## Common Use Cases

- **Cybersecurity**: reconnaissance for penetration testing, attack surface mapping, phishing-susceptibility assessment
- **Journalism/investigations**: verifying claims, tracing ownership structures, geolocating conflict footage
- **Corporate due diligence**: vetting business partners, M&A research, competitive intelligence
- **Physical security**: executive protection, threat assessment (identifying if someone's public footprint creates risk)
- **Law enforcement/fraud investigation**: within legal bounds, corroborating claims or locating assets
- **Personal digital hygiene**: auditing your own exposure — what an attacker could piece together about you

## A Word on Ethics/Legality

The line isn't "is this technically public" — it's about aggregation harm, consent, and jurisdiction. Compiling scattered public data into a dossier on a private individual can cross into stalking or harassment even if every individual piece was "public." Professional OSINT practitioners generally operate under scoped authorization (a client engagement, a legal investigation) rather than open-ended profiling of arbitrary people.

Here's OSINT reframed through a business development and revenue-growth lens — treating public information as a pipeline for finding, understanding, and winning customers.

## The Core Idea

For BD, OSINT means turning scattered public signals about prospects, markets, and competitors into **timing, targeting, and talking points** — knowing who to approach, when, and with what pitch, before you ever pick up the phone.

## Source Categories

**Direct sources** — what prospects and markets tell you outright:

- Company websites, "About Us," leadership pages, product pages
- Press releases, funding announcements, earnings calls (public companies)
- Job postings (a strong signal of growth areas, budget, and pain points)
- LinkedIn — org structure, recent hires/departures, employee growth rate
- Industry reports, trade publications, conference speaker lists

**Indirect sources** — signals companies don't realize they're broadcasting:

- Tech stack detection (via job postings or site-scanning tools) revealing what tools they use — and what gaps a competitor product could fill
- Review sites (G2, Capterra, Glassdoor) — complaints about a competitor are a sales opening; complaints about internal culture hint at initiatives, budget shifts, or hires
- SEC filings / Companies House — reveal financial health, new subsidiaries, M&A activity
- Patent filings — early signal of a company's next product direction
- Social media activity of decision-makers — what they're posting, sharing, or complaining about publicly
- Website changes over time (via Wayback Machine) — new markets entered, pricing changes, messaging pivots

## The Inference Layer (turning data into a pitch)

This is where OSINT becomes revenue-generating rather than just research:

1. **Buying-signal detection** — a company posting multiple job openings for "revenue operations" or "data engineer" often signals a coming tech purchase in that category.
2. **Trigger-event timing** — new leadership, funding rounds, office expansions, or M&A activity are proven moments when budgets loosen and vendors get reconsidered.
3. **Pain-point mapping** — cross-referencing Glassdoor complaints, G2 reviews of competitors, and support-forum questions to identify what a prospect is actively frustrated by.
4. **Org-chart reconstruction** — LinkedIn + company site bios let you map the real decision-making chain, not just the org chart title, so outreach goes to the right person the first time.
5. **Competitive displacement** — identifying a prospect's current vendor (via case studies, integrations, tech-stack tools) to build a pitch specifically around switching costs and gaps.
6. **Warm-path identification** — mutual connections, shared alumni networks, or overlapping conference attendance surfaced via LinkedIn/Twitter, turning a cold outreach into a warm one.
7. **Account prioritization/scoring** — combining company size, hiring velocity, funding stage, and tech stack signals into a lead score to focus effort on accounts most likely to convert now.

## Common Use Cases

- **Prospecting & lead generation**: building targeted account lists based on real-time signals rather than static firmographic data
- **Sales enablement**: arming reps with specific, relevant talking points (a recent funding round, a leadership change, a competitor complaint) instead of generic pitches
- **Account-based marketing (ABM)**: tailoring campaigns to a specific company's known initiatives and pain points
- **Competitive positioning**: understanding what competitors are winning/losing on to sharpen your own value proposition
- **Partnership/channel development**: identifying companies with complementary tech stacks or overlapping customer bases for co-sell opportunities
- **Renewal/expansion intelligence**: monitoring existing customers' hiring, funding, or strategic shifts to spot upsell timing
- **Market entry research**: sizing a new vertical or geography by aggregating public data before committing sales resources

## Practical Workflow Example

A rep targeting mid-market SaaS companies might: pull a list of companies that raised Series B in the last 90 days → check their job postings for a specific role your product supports → check G2 for what CRM/tool they currently use → check LinkedIn for the VP who'd own the buying decision → reach out referencing the funding news and the specific hiring signal. Each step alone is trivial; stacked together, it's a highly qualified, well-timed, personalized outreach — built entirely from public data.

## A Note on Boundaries

Even in BD, there's a line between diligent research and creepy over-personalization. Referencing a funding round or job posting reads as informed; referencing someone's personal social media activity in a cold pitch often reads as invasive. Good BD-OSINT stays anchored to business-relevant signals.

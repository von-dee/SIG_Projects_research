The crush reject is the retained half of the crush-and-split step — the material that *didn't* go on to be pulverised into pulp. It's not waste in the trash sense; it's an archive.

**Physically**, after the dried sample is crushed to a coarser fraction (a few millimeters, not powder), a splitter divides it into two roughly equal streams. One stream continues forward to pulverising. The other — the reject — goes into a labeled bag or tray and into long-term storage, usually for a fixed retention period set by the lab or the client (commonly one to several years, sometimes indefinitely for mineral resource work).

**Why it's kept, not discarded:**

- **Re-assay / check assay** — if a result comes back anomalous, or QA/QC flags a failed standard or duplicate nearby, the lab can re-split and re-pulverise the crush reject rather than going back to the original core, which may already be logged, sampled elsewhere, or partially destroyed.
- **Umpire / referee analysis** — in disputes (common in resource estimation, ore reconciliation, or contractual grade disputes), an independent third-party lab needs untouched material at a known stage of prep. The reject is exactly that.
- **Coarser-fraction test work** — metallurgical or mineralogical studies sometimes specifically want the coarse crush fraction rather than pulp.

**In the graph model**, this is why the crush-split step has to be a genuine split edge with two live output nodes rather than a discard. The reject node stays `status: active/archived` — it's queryable, it has its own mass, and any future work on it (a re-split, a re-crush) becomes a new edge hanging off that node, extending the same graph rather than starting a new record. If the schema modeled rejects as "consumed" or didn't track them as nodes at all, you'd lose the ability to answer "can we still check this result?" — which, for anything with financial or regulatory weight riding on the grade, is often the whole point of keeping the graph in the first place.

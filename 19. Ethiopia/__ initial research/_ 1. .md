Here's a practical technology architecture for this — I'd treat it as **one integrated concession-monitoring system with four data feeds**, rather than four separate tools, since all of them ultimately need to roll up to the same thing: a per-concession compliance and productivity record. Worth flagging upfront: Ghana's Minerals Commission has already built something very close to this nationally — a Mine Repository and Tracking Control Centre that tracks the movement of earthmoving equipment in mining concessions, using 4G trackers on excavators in the artisanal and small-scale mining sector — and a newer platform, AIMCEPS, that integrates equipment tracking, automated geofencing, AI-powered drone surveillance and centralized command-and-control, piloted with the Commission itself. It's worth checking whether AAC's system should feed into or align with that national standard rather than build a fully parallel one — it'll matter for permit reporting either way.

## 1. Tracking lent excavators + their geotagged permits

- **Hardwired telematics units** (not just battery GPS pucks) on every excavator — these tap the machine's CAN bus/J1939 data stream to pull real engine hours, fault codes, and fuel burn versus idle time directly from the machine's ECU, not just a location pin.
- **Cellular + satellite hybrid connectivity**, since concessions are often rural with patchy network coverage — cellular for normal reporting, satellite fallback so you never lose visibility when a unit goes into a dead zone.
- **Geofencing against the actual permit polygon**, not just a radius — import each concession's licensed boundary (from the Minerals Commission cadastre or your own surveyed coordinates) as the geofence shape. This does double duty: it confirms the excavator is working inside the licensed area (compliance) and flags if it's moved outside it (theft, sub-leasing, or encroachment into someone else's concession).
- **An asset-lending ledger layered on top of the GPS data** — since these machines aren't owned by the people operating them, you need a check-out/check-in record tying each machine's serial/asset ID to a specific concession holder's permit ID, loan date, and condition report. The GPS trail becomes evidence for that ledger, not a replacement for it.

## 2. Fuel usage of the excavators

- Where the excavator has a modern ECU, fuel burn comes for free through the same J1939 telematics feed as above. For older machines, retrofit an **in-tank capacitive or ultrasonic fuel level sensor** — capacitive sensors with coaxial probes provide fine-grained fuel level, volume, and temperature readings with no moving parts, and ultrasonic sensors can flag fuel theft in real time once integrated with the telematics platform.
- **Sudden-drop tamper alerts**: a fast fuel-level drop while the engine is off (siphoning) versus a gradual drop while running (normal consumption) should trigger different alert types. This matters more here than on an owned fleet, precisely because the equipment is out with third parties.
- **Fuel reconciliation, not just fuel tracking**: if AAC supplies the diesel, compare litres dispensed at your depot against litres consumed per the sensor against engine hours worked. A gap between "fuel out" and "fuel burned for hours logged" is your fraud/leakage signal, and it turns fuel data into a per-concession cost-per-tonne-moved metric rather than a standalone log.
- Idle-time reporting matters economically too — industry data suggests 10–30% of heavy equipment fuel consumption comes from non-productive idling, and telematics typically cuts that by 10–15% once operators know it's visible.

## 3. Tailings volume estimation and bi-weekly evolution tracking

This is the piece with the most moving parts, so the workflow matters more than any single tool:

- **Baseline surface**: your geologists establish the reference model first — ground survey points, drill logs, cross-sections, an initial DEM (digital elevation model) of the tailings/waste extent per concession. This is the "zero point" everything else is measured against.
- **Bi-weekly drone capture**: RGB photogrammetry (Pix4D, DroneDeploy, Propeller Aero, Agisoft Metashape) for open ground, or LiDAR where vegetation obstructs the surface, since LiDAR can penetrate vegetation and obstructions to still deliver accurate elevation data. Flown with RTK/PPK GPS correction rather than plain onboard GPS — with consistent altitude, sufficient image overlap, and RTK corrections, drone photogrammetry delivers stockpile volume accuracy within 1–2% of ground survey methods, and most mining and aggregate sites already fly on a weekly-to-monthly cadence for exactly this kind of volumetric tracking, so bi-weekly is well within normal practice.
- **Volumetric differencing software** (Propeller Aero, Pix4D, DroneDeploy, or Birdi) compares each new flight's surface to the geologist's baseline and to the prior flight, producing cut/fill maps and a tonnage estimate once your geologists apply a measured bulk-density figure. A reconciliation variance of 2–5% is generally considered acceptable for drone photogrammetry paired with tested density inputs.
- **Standardize the output format** (point clouds, DEMs, CSV volumes) and store it against the concession's permit ID in the same GIS the excavator and truck data live in, so a "tailings evolution over time" chart is just a query, not a separate manual exercise.
- For very remote or numerous concessions, consider a rotating drone-pilot schedule or contracted regional operators rather than in-house pilots at every site — cost and logistics usually decide this more than technology does.

## 4. Geotagging dump trucks: CIL plant ↔ concession

- Same telematics category as the excavators, but tuned for road movement rather than idle machine monitoring — ignition-triggered GPS units reporting on a short interval. Real-time GPS with roughly 30-second update intervals, geofence alerts on entry/exit, unauthorized movement detection, and after-hours operation alerts is the standard baseline here.
- **Geofence both ends and the corridor**: a fence at the CIL plant gate, a fence at each concession's loading point, and (if you want route-deviation alerts) a corridor buffer along the approved haul route. A truck that leaves the corridor mid-trip is either a breakdown, a diversion, or unauthorized dumping — worth flagging either way.
- **Tie trips to tonnage**: if the CIL plant has (or can get) a weighbridge, pairing each geofence-triggered "trip event" with a weighbridge ticket gives you verified tonnage moved per concession, not just a truck movement log.
- Optional second factor: RFID tags or ANPR (license-plate recognition) cameras at the plant gate, useful as an audit trail if GPS data is ever disputed.

## Tying it together

The common thread across all four is that every reading — GPS ping, fuel litre, drone-derived tonne, truck trip — should carry the **concession permit ID** as a foreign key. That's what lets you build one dashboard (built on something like ArcGIS/QGIS + PostGIS, or a COTS platform like the ones above) with a live map and per-concession cards showing: equipment on site, fuel burned, tailings trend line, and truck trips logged — essentially the same "control room" model the Minerals Commission runs nationally. A few practical calls to make early:

- **Connectivity strategy** (cellular-only vs. cellular+satellite) — this is usually the single biggest cost and reliability driver for concessions in remote terrain.
- **Build vs. partner** — building this from scratch across four domains (telematics, drone GIS, fuel hardware, fleet routing) is a lot of integration work; partnering with an existing platform (AIMCEPS, or piecing together Geotab/Hapn for fleet + Propeller/Pix4D for drone) may get you live faster.
- **Offline-first design** for edge devices, so data buffers locally and syncs when a concession comes back into coverage instead of silently dropping readings.

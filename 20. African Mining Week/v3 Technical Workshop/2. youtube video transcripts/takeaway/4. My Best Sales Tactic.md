# Reference Guide: Ghost Products and Trust-Based Selling

## Main Takeaways

- **Give trust before you ask for the sale.** Acting visibly in the customer's interest, even at a small cost to yourself, makes them trust you faster.
- **Use "ghost products" (sacrificial lambs) as concessions.** Send customers elsewhere for a few items, or tell them to skip an item, so they believe you aren't just selling for your own gain.
- **Give away the low-margin items and keep the high-margin ones.** The concession should cost you little, and the trust it earns carries over to the products you want to sell.
- **Intention is what separates help from manipulation.** The speaker argues this works best when you believe in what you sell and the customer really needs it.
- **Prescribe, don't just pitch.** Paint a picture of life with the benefits, then tell the customer exactly how to use each product.
- **Attach new habits to existing ones.** Tying product use to something the customer already does every day makes it more likely to stick.
- **Close with low friction.** After the prescription, make buying a simple yes (e.g., "use the card on file?") rather than a new decision.
- **Handle price objections without devaluing your products.** Rank the items by importance, remove the least essential one, and restate the problem it solves if they hesitate.
- **Reframe spending around the customer's "old identity."** Habits they'll drop (going out, drinking, smoking) free up money for the new routine.
- **Build skill through high-volume, low-ticket repetition.** Many small transactions teach selling faster than any course.
- **Aim to end up "shoulder to shoulder."** Move from opposite sides of the table to looking at the decision together.

## Takeaway-to-Example Map

| Key Takeaway                                      | Real-World Example from the Text                                                                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Give trust before asking for the sale             | When a key product ran out, he told customers they could get it cheaper at Costco. They thanked him, then bought everything else he recommended.                                         |
| Use ghost products as concessions                 | He started pointing customers to two products elsewhere ("save you 10 or 20 bucks") and eventually stopped stocking them because they worked so well as trust-builders.                  |
| Give away low-margin items, keep high-margin ones | He advises sending the lowest-margin products "across the street" and keeping the high-margin ones to sell himself.                                                                      |
| Intention separates help from manipulation        | He says he influences people with positive intent, and applies the tactic only when he believes they need the product.                                                                   |
| Prescribe, don't just pitch                       | He explained each product's benefits and told customers exactly how to take it: "two of these in the morning," "three of these next to this," "put this in your car."                    |
| Attach new habits to existing ones                | He asked what customers do every morning, then told a customer who brushes her teeth to keep the product next to her toothbrush, with tape marked "2" on it.                             |
| Close with low friction                           | After the prescription: "Did you want to use the card you have on file?" The customer only has to say yes, a "one-click upsell."                                                         |
| Handle price objections without devaluing         | If the customer is on a budget, he removes the "lowest" item. If they balk, he says they'd need to replace it with, say, "a pound of broccoli," then reintroduces the problem it solves. |
| Reframe spending around the old identity          | He asks what they'll stop doing (going out, drinking, smoking), asks how much that costs, and says, "We just found some money."                                                          |
| Build skill through low-ticket repetition         | He suggests embedding in businesses like car washes, massage, nails, or hair to get "rep after rep," and says it made higher-ticket sales feel "a breeze."                               |
| Apply the tactic in services                      | A web design seller can say a task is "not our core capability," that it would cost more, and that a contact in the Philippines can do it.                                               |
| End up shoulder to shoulder                       | A salesperson can lean to the customer's side of the table ("I'll hook you up with a number of a guy") so both are looking at the decision together.                                     |

**A caution:** the speaker's own example in the last row involves appearing to be on the customer's side while still pursuing the sale. That is where his intention argument gets tested. The approach is on firmest ground when the concessions are real (the customer truly can get the item cheaper elsewhere) and the products you recommend truly serve them.

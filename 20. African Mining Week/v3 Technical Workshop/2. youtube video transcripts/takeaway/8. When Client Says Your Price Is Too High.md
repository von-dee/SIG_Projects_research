# Reference Guide: Negotiating Price and Scope as a Video Freelancer

## Main Takeaways

- **Probe the goal before discussing price.** Chris asks what outcome Mo wants and quantifies it, so the budget is judged against results rather than in a vacuum.
- **Translate outcomes into revenue to test proportionality.** Connecting the video to expected income shows whether the budget matches the value.
- **A small budget signals low importance.** If a client says they can't spend more, the project may not be a real priority, and it may not be worth taking.
- **People spend on what they value.** Comparing a client's budget to their other purchases reveals how much they truly care.
- **Be wary of "video envy."** Clients who copy competitors without a clear purpose tend to be poor clients and get poor results.
- **Underpriced jobs cost more than they pay.** Low-paying work often brings extra revisions and long conversations, and it never gets to the client's real needs.
- **You need a runway to say no.** Financial cushion is what makes it possible to walk away from a bad deal.
- **You didn't have the money before the call.** Don't let one lead push you into a position where you feel you must close.
- **Everything is negotiable, including scope.** If the client doesn't meet your price, you don't have to meet their deliverable.
- **Offer clear alternatives.** Presenting two options (flat fee vs. revenue share) lets the client decide, and framing the choice around risk steers them toward the fixed fee.
- **Anchor with confidence and a firm minimum.** Naming a minimum ($4,000) and holding it moved the client from $1,000 to $4,000.
- **Let the client run the numbers.** When a client sees the potential return, the higher fixed price starts to look prudent.
- **Protect yourself in revenue-share deals.** Caps, time limits, and honesty-based measurement are all points to negotiate.

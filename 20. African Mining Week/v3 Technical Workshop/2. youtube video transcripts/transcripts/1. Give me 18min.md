
I analyzed 78 of the most successful speakers on the planet to find out how they tell stories. After watching hundreds of their videos, I found that it all comes down to one thing, and it's the one thing most people don't use. Once you know it, you'll tell stories like the top 1%.

Ask people what the single most important element of great storytelling is, and you'll hear a range of answers: a hook, conflict, authenticity, emotion. All of them are true, and a story like the one below shows why.

A woman is on a gym machine when a man who looks like Thor's personal trainer stares at her. She wonders if she's doing something wrong. She summons the courage to ask if everything is okay, and he says he's just in his own world. She gets off the machine, and he approaches her. "You've been working out here a long time," he says. She thinks, *oh my god, he's hitting on me.* Then he says he's a trainer and wonders if she wants some pointers. *Oh no, he's not hitting on me. He just wants a job.* She mentions she had a baby seven weeks ago, and he says, "Oh, trying to lose the baby weight?" She musters her dignity and says, "No, Brandon, I'm just trying to regain my strength." He says "Great" and walks away. And she cried a little.

That story has emotion, authenticity, and detail, but underneath all of them is one underlying theme.

## The helicopter and the trenches

Imagine a war movie that opens with a helicopter shot from high above. You see tanks advancing and soldiers lining up. It's interesting for about two seconds, and then you want to be taken down there. You want to see the mud on their faces and be part of the action.

Our stories often stay stuck in that helicopter view: "I had a difficult problem. It was tough, but I overcame it." That's high level. The best storytellers zoom into the trenches, into the moment where something is actually happening.

Zooming in comes down to four steps, which I call **PAST**: Place, Action, Speech, and Thoughts.

## P is for Place

A common mistake at the start of a story is being too descriptive: "It was a warm Sunday afternoon, birds were singing, there was the smell of lavender in the air." Start like that and you've lost your listeners before you've begun.

Instead, state the place early. "Two weeks ago I'm sitting in my apartment in Amsterdam, looking at my laptop." Or: "Last July I'm standing in front of the conference room, taking a deep breath." You're already in it, and a picture is forming in the listener's mind.

You don't need to describe the room in detail, with its wooden table, flat screen, and chairs. It doesn't matter whether listeners picture your conference room or their own. They just need to see some conference room. State the place and the image appears.

## A is for Action

The most common mistake I've heard across more than 2,000 stories is giving too much context: "I worked at this company for three years, it was a good company, I liked my manager," and on and on. By then you're thinking, *where is the story?*

The better way is to start right in the action, with forward momentum. "Two weeks ago I'm in my apartment in Amsterdam when this notification pops up." Or: "I'm at airport security, putting my bag on the belt, when someone calls my name: Phillip."

To get the most from this, use action verbs. *I walk. I shout. I bike. I open the door. I close it.* Each one creates a movie in your listener's mind, and the more visual it is, the more people are drawn into the moment.

Here's how that sounds in practice. A woman describes it like this: "She's got her arms wide open and she's smiling at me. She's walking towards me, and as I start walking towards her, my heart drops. I take deep breaths while trying to smile, because I don't know her name. As we get closer, she says 'Hey,' and I say 'Hey.' And through the whole conversation I can tell she noticed I didn't know her name, and I just wanted the floor to eat me alive."

We were right there in the moment, with everything unfolding before our eyes. One small suggestion would be to also anchor the story with a place and a date. Once you state a time and location, people accept it as a true story, whereas leaving them out invites doubt.

Another woman begins: "Last Friday I was in Mexico with my boyfriend and some of his friends when I started talking with his friend Luca. On Sunday I was supposed to meet Lucas, a similar name, at a beach club. I didn't realize Luca and Lucas were two different people, so I accidentally invited Luca instead of Lucas, and it got awkward." She jumped straight into the action, which is great. The improvement is to share what was said in the moment: "I looked at Lucas and said, 'I'll see you on Sunday.' And he said, 'What?'"

## S is for Speech

This is the technique I use the most, and it makes any story far more interesting. Most stories have more than one character, such as a coworker, a friend, or a manager. To make your story come alive, share the exact words spoken at the crucial moment.

An amateur says, "My manager was very upset with me." That isn't very interesting. Much better: "In that moment my manager called me and said, 'Phillip, what the hell was that? You were all over the place in that presentation.'" Likewise, "My customer was very upset" becomes, "My customer called and said, 'That technology you just sold us messed up our entire production system. This is terrible.'"

Take "My mom was very happy." A volunteer tells it this way: her mother was having a birthday party and didn't know her son was coming. He knocked, she opened the door, and he saw the smile on her face. But what did she actually say? Something like, "I'm really happy you're here, my son." Or perhaps, "Why are you here? That's crazy!" Whatever it is, share the exact words.

If you worry you won't remember what was said, remember that my own memory is lousy too. If you can't recall the dialogue, use the words that *could* have been said. You have some creative freedom here.

Here's one more example. A woman says: "Two weeks ago, before coming to Bali, I broke up with my boyfriend of five years. It was a Sunday evening. I came into the kitchen and said, 'Hey, it's over. I just can't anymore.' And his only response was, 'Okay, what are we going to do with the house?'" She expected something more, some emotion, but he moved on to the practical questions: sell it, or do something else with it? By the time he got to the emotional part, it was too late for her.

The tip here is to find the most important line, which is the boyfriend's first response, and milk it. Pause more before it, and build anticipation by sharing what you were thinking: *I wonder what he's going to say. Does he want to win me back?* The bigger the buildup, the stronger the contrast. When she retold it, she did exactly that: "I was really expecting some emotion. I was expecting something to revive the relationship. And his only response was: 'Okay, what are we going to do with the house?'" It landed far better. The one improvement left was to pause even longer after his line and let it sink in.

## T is for Thoughts

The last technique is the one that makes any story emotional. It's how you truly connect with your listeners, and many of you were already doing it unconsciously.

Bad storytellers give a summary: "I had a difficult situation with my manager." Amateur storytellers describe the emotion: "My manager was very frustrated." That's slightly better, but still flat. Great storytellers share the exact thoughts of that moment: "I thought, *babe, he's going to hate me for this.*"

We have thousands of thoughts every day, and many of them are weird, anxious, hopeful, or full of plans. Sharing some of them makes your story far more interesting.

Take "I was nervous about the meeting." Boring. Try instead: "I thought, *this is going to be terrible. They're going to tear my presentation apart. I'll be crying in this meeting.*"

Take "I was excited about my date." One volunteer first said she was looking at outfits, couldn't decide, and was anxious and wanted everything perfect. That's still summary. When asked to stand in front of the wardrobe and share her thoughts, she said: "I was looking in the mirror, and in my head I was thinking, *is he going to be funny? Am I going to laugh at his jokes? Are we going to be a match?* There were so many questions in my mind." That's exactly it: raw, unfiltered thoughts as they arise in the moment.

Take "I was embarrassed about the money." A volunteer told of a date with her crush who, in the end, said she had no interest in him. Then the bill came, and his first thought was, *should I pay for it or not?* In the end, he told her he'd just pay for himself. The thought made the story funny and real.

Give us the raw, unfiltered version, not the polished one. Nobody thinks, "This represents a supreme opportunity for my career progression." Our actual thoughts sound more like, *I am dead here.*

## Putting it all together

Those are the four techniques that matter most for zooming into the moment: Place, Action, Speech, Thoughts. Now watch how a comedian uses all four in about 30 seconds.

He says he was on a plane to Russia when the engine exploded. He was looking out the window as the entire engine turned into a fireball, and he seemed to be the only one who saw it. He felt like he'd already died and gone to heaven, because no one said anything. He was screaming at the top of his lungs, "What the hell is going on here?" The flight attendant came out and said, "We seem to have a slight problem." A Russian passenger finally asked, "What is the problem?" "Well, we lost one of our engines." The man asked, "How many engines did we have?" "Well, we had two. Now we have one."

Looking at it through PAST:

- **Place:** Russia, and an airplane.
- **Action:** He starts by looking out the window and seeing the explosion, right in the action.
- **Speech:** The flight attendant's understated line and the Russian passenger's questions. He even imitates them, taking over the whole conversation.
- **Thoughts:** "I felt like I'd already died and gone to heaven."

All four elements appear in a story that takes only 30 seconds, and it's incredible. You can turn any average moment into a remarkable story by zooming into it. And you zoom in with the PAST framework: place, action, speech, and thoughts. Use it, and your stories will be unrecognizable.

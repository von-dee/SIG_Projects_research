**The first technique is location: say where you are.** The best stories start by stating where you are physically. For example: "Two weeks ago, I'm sitting on my couch in my living room, taking a deep breath." Or: "September 2019, I'm standing in front of the conference room, ready to walk inside."

Why does this matter? The moment you say "conference room" or "living room," your audience starts to visualize it. They think, "Oh yes, I know a conference room," and they picture their own version of it. Beginner storytellers often make the mistake of giving too many details, such as a big table, a television, and a wooden floor. Don't do that. It doesn't matter. Just state the location, and as long as your listeners can visualize it, they'll fill in the details themselves.

**The second technique is actions: what are you doing in that specific moment?** Instead of giving way too much context about random things that don't matter, say what you're doing right then. That could be walking, biking, shouting, reading, or waiting. Just state the verbs. For example: "I'm in my office. I open my laptop and start reading a message from my manager." Or: "Two weeks ago, I'm at the airport, waiting in line at security."

Why does this matter? Actions bring forward momentum and take the listener right into the moment. Your audience immediately knows you won't waste their time, because you're taking them straight to the most important part of the story.

**The third technique is thoughts: what are you thinking?** As humans, we have thousands upon thousands of thoughts every single day, and many of them are hopes, dreams, plans, fears, worries, and crazy ideas. Share some of those thoughts from the crucial moment of your story.

For example, instead of saying "I was excited to meet my crush," say, "I thought, ah, this will be so cool! Finally I can see her after all that time." Or instead of saying "I was very disappointed about that presentation," say, "I thought, oh man, this is bad. Everyone will think I'm stupid now. I can never go back there." Do you see the difference? It's a tiny tweak, but it makes any story more interesting.

A common mistake is sharing thoughts that sound too professional or too intellectual, like "I thought this represented a supreme opportunity." Would your thoughts actually sound like that? Probably not. So give us the raw, unfiltered thoughts, the slightly ranty, juicy, a little bit neurotic ones. That makes your story much more relatable.

**The fourth technique is emotions: what are you feeling?** The best stories are emotional and take listeners on an emotional journey. With the previous technique, you already know one way to make a story more emotional, but there's another. The simplest way is to just name the emotion: "I was disappointed," "I was relieved," "I was happy." That's pretty standard, and it's what most people do, but it's not ideal because it isn't visual. When someone says "I was disappointed," you can't really picture it.

The better way is to show the emotion, meaning how it looks on the body and the face. Instead of saying "I was relieved," say, "In that moment, I leaned backward and let out a big sigh." Instead of saying "He was anxious," say, "He kept tapping his pen on the table and glancing up at the clock every few seconds." Do you see the difference? Showing the emotion makes it much more visual and takes us into the specific moment of the story.

Or instead of saying "My manager was very happy with my work," say, "In that moment, my manager looked at me and said, 'Wow, that was really the best presentation you've ever given.'" Dialogue is such a simple tool for making any story more interesting, and it's the one I use the most.

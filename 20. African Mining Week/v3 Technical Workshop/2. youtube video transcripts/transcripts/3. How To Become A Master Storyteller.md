# Six Storytelling Techniques That Will Improve Your Content

Today we're talking about storytelling. If you want your content to perform better, you need to learn how to tell better stories. In this video, I'm going to walk you through my six favorite storytelling techniques, and after watching, I guarantee you'll think differently about the way you write scripts and make videos.

By the way, I'm Callaway. I've pulled over a billion views on short-form video, I've got 500,000 followers, and I think about this stuff all the time. Let's dive in.

## 1. The Dance

Tip number one is what I call **the dance**. All great stories are a dance between context and conflict. You give a little context: the character is on a mission, doing something. Then, oh no, here comes a conflict. You give a little more context, the character solves that first conflict and they're on their way. And then, oh no, another conflict.

This dance is how you keep the viewer locked in. It could be a feature film or a short-form video; the dance is always there. It works because conflict creates open loops in the brain, and context helps close those loops.

The best explanation I've seen of how to actually implement this comes from Matt Stone and Trey Parker, the creators of *South Park*, in a talk they gave at NYU. They said it took them a long time to learn a really simple rule. Take the beats of your outline. If the words "and then" belong between those beats, you've got something pretty boring. What should sit between every beat is either "therefore" or "but." This happens, *therefore* this happens, *but* this happens, *therefore* this happens. They'll sometimes literally write it out to make sure they're doing it, and that's why the show feels like each moment causes the next, with a complication at every turn.

Tactically, it's as simple as using the words "but" and "therefore" consistently throughout your script. That creates open conflict loops. If you use "and then," it feels like you're piling on detail after detail after detail, and the viewer loses interest.

Let me show you one of my best-performing videos. You'll notice there are four "but, then" conflict loops in the first 30 seconds:

*"Something crazy is happening with Stanley Cups. You ever seen one of these? Stanley Cup is racking up 6.7 billion views. This 40-ounce Stanley Quencher has become the Louis Vuitton of drinkware. It's a status symbol. New releases are selling out in seconds, people are flipping them for hundreds on eBay, and Stanley, the company, 10x'd its revenue in four years off this single product. But the real question is: how did they do it? How did Stanley Cups go from a construction worker's thermos to the dream Christmas gift? It's a wild story. In 2019, Stanley was actually about to discontinue the cup, but this group of mom bloggers, The Buy Guide, knew they were making a huge mistake. So they cut a special affiliate deal with Stanley to bulk order 5,000 cups, with a twist: Stanley had to make them in pastel colors. Those 5,000 cups sold out in five days and completely changed the company's philosophy on design. But it gets even better. A few months ago, Stanley had the burning car moment..."*

I'm consistently setting up these head fakes to keep the storyline moving. Use this "but, therefore" framework next time you're writing a script, and I guarantee it'll level up that video.

## 2. Rhythm

Tip number two is **rhythm**. I talk about this a lot, but there's a natural, subconscious pacing and rhythm that is soothing to the brain. You can hear it in my delivery: there's an ebb and flow, and the rhythm of the syllables when I talk is pleasant to listen to. I used to be a rapper, and I was a drummer growing up, so this pacing is kind of embedded in me, which is probably why I'm naturally good at talking this way. But there's a tactical way anyone can improve their rhythm.

Take a look at this passage by the legendary author Gary Provost. It starts with a run of short, five-word sentences. They're fine on their own, but stacked together they become monotonous. The writing gets boring, the sound of it drones like a stuck record, and the ear demands some variety. Then Provost changes things up. He varies the sentence length, and suddenly the writing has music in it. He mixes short sentences with medium ones, and every so often, when the reader is rested, he delivers a long sentence that builds with the energy of a crescendo: the roll of the drums, the crash of the cymbals. Those are sounds that say, "Listen to this, it's important." His advice is to write with a combination of short, medium, and long sentences, to create a sound that pleases the reader's ear. Don't just write words. Write music.

See how, when all the sentences in a row are about the same length, it creates this monotonous predictability? Subconsciously, people get turned off by it, and that's what makes them churn to the next video. What you want is a variety of sentence lengths and syllable combinations to create an unpredictable rhythm.

If you look at my script document, I write every sentence on a separate line. If you look straight down the page, it forms a jagged edge, and that confirms I'm using different-length sentences. Next time you're writing a script, look for this. If all your sentences seem to end at around the same point, you don't have enough diversity, and it's going to feel like monotonous plodding.

## 3. Tone

Lesson three is **tone**. The most successful creators in every discipline are the ones with the most conversational tone. It really feels like you're right in the room with them. Emma Chamberlain ascended to stardom so quickly because she was naturally amazing at this. Casey Neistat is another one who's really good at it. When you watch his videos and listen to him talk, it feels like he's right in the room with you.

The person I learned the most about this from was Steve Jobs. Watch his original keynote from the first iPhone launch, and you'll see he's amazing at creating a conversational room, like you and him are just golf buddies shooting the breeze. This is very intentional, and it took years of practice for him to hone, though he's also very gifted at it.

By doing this, Emma, Casey, Steve, and others break down the conscious barrier of personal identity. Instead of asking yourself, "Am I getting sold to?", you get out of your own head and feel like you're in a conversation, like you'd have to answer a question they ask you directly. You forget where you are, and that's the magic of it.

I'm still working on this myself. I've only been making content for about 18 months, and if you go back to my first 50 videos, it really does feel like I'm talking *at* you instead of *with* you. The best tactical way to improve, other than just putting in a hundred reps, is to write and film your videos as if you're talking to one close friend. If you have to, print out a picture of them and tape it to the bottom of your camera lens so you're literally looking at them as you record. When you write your scripts, write as if you're typing a text or recording an audio note directly to them. Over time, you'll get better at this conversational quality, and it will feel like a one-on-one conversation. That's how you break down the barrier. Tone is one of those things that inevitably improves over time: the more you film, the more you forget that the camera is just a camera.

## 4. Direction

Tip number four is **direction**. This may seem counterintuitive, but the best place to start when writing a story is the end. Figure out what the end is going to be, then work backwards from there.

I like to call the last line of my script "the last dab," because I want it to be so memorable that if it's all someone heard, they'd be willing to share it with a friend. In short-form video especially, the video is designed to loop, so the last line is actually a setup for the first lines as it replays. I think of it a lot like baseball: the nine hitter is supposed to set the table for the top of the order. It's exactly the same with the last line or two of your video.

When you're writing your script, focus on where you want to take the viewer. What do you want to leave them with? What do you want the last thing they hear to be? Then work backwards from there and build up the dance in the middle.

Someone who does this extremely well is Christopher Nolan. Think about *Tenet*, *Inception*, *Interstellar*. These are incredibly complicated plotlines, and he had to work out the end before he could build up the conflict in the middle. You're probably not shooting feature films, but the same logic applies to all types of content. Usually, when I'm writing scripts, I'll write the first and last line, create a bunch of space in between, and then fill it in. That's a good tactical way to get better at this.

## 5. Story Lenses

Tip number five is a concept I call **story lenses**, and it's a way to get better at producing unique content for social media. In today's day and age, finding a cool topic is not enough. Unless you're super niched down, there are likely dozens of other people talking about the same topic. So how do you differentiate?

A story lens is your unique angle or spin on a particular story. Imagine a beam of light coming across the screen. The naked beam is what everyone looks at, and everyone sees the same thing: a plain white beam. But as soon as you put a lens or a prism in front of it, what people see looks different from the main beam. Think of that prism as your story lens. It's your unique fingerprint, the way you uniquely tell the story about that topic.

Let's take an example. When Taylor Swift went to the Super Bowl, the most common lenses were what she was wearing, when she was arriving, or what her facial reaction was to something. A lot of people covered those exact same stories. A less common lens was making a prediction about what might happen. Fewer people did that. But an even less common lens was the business impact she was driving for the NFL by being at the game. That's the lens I chose, because I thought I could be a category of one. That's why I ultimately made that video, and it pulled a million views. It was different. My story lens was something unique that people hadn't seen before.

## 6. The Hook

The last one, tip number six, is **the hook**. I left it for last because everybody always talks about the hook first, and that makes sense. It is very important: if people churn on the hook, the rest of what I've talked about doesn't really matter. I have two ways to level up your hooks.

The first is that your first line should be as punchy and as indicative of the plot as possible. If the video is about your best gardening techniques, the hook should be some derivative of "These are the best gardening techniques for X," or "I've got these gardening techniques," or "This is a gardening technique you should study." You shouldn't open with an opaque line like "Wait till you see this" or "You're never going to believe this," because if the first line doesn't immediately grab the viewer in short-form video, you're dead.

The second point, which is extremely powerful and something I overlooked for a long time, is that **visual hooks are ten times more effective than audio-only hooks.** What does this mean? If you're on screen saying something, but the only thing the viewer sees is your mouth moving and maybe the captions dancing, that's going to be far less effective than putting a visual on the screen for them to look at and react to. People's eyes perceive faster than their ears can hear, so you want to complement what you're saying with a visual.

Somebody who's really good at this is my friend Kevin from Epic Gardening. If you watch his videos, he immediately shows a visual that confirms what he's talking about. In one about strawberries, before I hear anything he says, I see a strawberry on screen, vivid red. Immediately I know the video is going to be about strawberries, and if I like strawberries, I'm going to stick around to see what he says. I've started testing this myself, and the visual hook has been super helpful at retaining viewers.

So, in summary, when you're thinking about your hook: get to the point, and show while you tell.

I want to tell you about the most powerful sales tactic I've ever learned. I didn't learn it from a book. I learned it by accident, and it will be included in the $100 Million Sales Book when it comes out, so you heard it here first. It's a concept you can use as a salesperson to gain trust. I stumbled into it, and after I made this one switch, I ended up selling 100% of the people I talked to. I was also able to teach it to people who had never sold before, and they were closing 80 to 90 percent of the people who came through the door. Mind you, this was a retail environment selling physical products.

So, rewind the clock. I was selling supplements at my gym. The way it worked was that we'd sell some sort of service package, and then we'd do a nutrition orientation, where we set people up with their meal plans and then made product recommendations. Normally I sold pretty well, but I always wanted to sell more and get better.

We had just run a big launch for a new challenge, and a hundred new customers came in. I ran out of one of the key products. The ladies who came in during the first half of the day were able to get everything, but their friends, who had signed up with them, came in during the second half. They'd say, "Hey, my friend Sandy told me I need X, Y, and Z," and I'd have to say, "Oh, we don't have Z anymore." It was really awkward.

So, rather than trying to skirt around a clear item on the list that I didn't have, I said, "By the way, you can get this one cheaper down the street at Costco, so you don't need to get it from me. Mine is a little better, but I think this one will get the job done. You can get the better one after you've done the program." And they said, "Oh, thanks, that was cool." After I made that one crossout, I said, "But you do want this and this from us," and they said, "Okay, cool."

As soon as I made that switch, saying "you don't need to buy this one, you can get it over there," everyone bought what I recommended afterward. I thought, whoa. So I leaned into it. I wondered if I could do more of that to sell even more on the back side. Then I had two products where I'd say, "You can get this and this over there. Get this brand, it should be around this price, and it'll save you ten or twenty bucks." And they'd say, "Wow, even better."

Then I remember looking at my list and seeing a mass gainer on it for a lady who obviously wasn't trying to gain mass. I said, "You're not trying to gain mass, are you?" She said no. I said, "You can just cross that out, don't worry about it." So I'd given her a recommendation for two things she could get for cheaper, and I'd told her not to worry about a third. Then I said, "I need you to take two of these in the morning next to this, take three of these next to this, and put this one in your car so you always have it with you." I closed everyone.

What that gave birth to was something I used to call sacrificial lambs, but now call ghost products, because it sounds better. Over time, I ended up not even carrying the products I was recommending people buy elsewhere, because they were such a powerful tool for gaining the other person's trust. It's an incredibly powerful tactic.

Like all persuasion, the difference between manipulation and help is intention. If you want to help someone, you're still influencing them, but with positive intent. If you change someone's behavior with negative intent, that's manipulation. If you believe in the things you sell, you can create an environment where someone trusts you faster by giving them a reason to trust you. That means you acted in their self-interest rather than your own, so they feel you're not trying to take advantage of them. If you know they need this stuff, but they don't yet know they need it, you can make a concession using a ghost product, a sacrificial lamb, to gain trust faster.

If you feel uncomfortable not having the product on your menu, feel free to carry it and never sell it. But I realized quickly that I was never selling it and always giving those ones away. If you're going to use this tactic, my recommendation is to make the products you send across the street the lowest-margin ones. Gain trust by giving away the low-margin stuff, and keep the high-margin stuff for yourself, so that people trust you and then buy that.

If you're in an environment where you sell multiple products, this has been the most effective way I've learned to sell. What you want to do is give someone a vision of what their life is going to be like when they're experiencing the benefits, and explain exactly how to take the product before you make the ask.

Here's what we would do. I'd say, "This one is going to help you with benefit, benefit, benefit. The way I need you to take it is two of these in the morning." Then I ask a question: "What do you do every day, no matter what, in the morning? Do you wake up and smoke a cigarette? Drink a cup of coffee? Take a shower? Brush your teeth? What's something you do every morning?" They say, "Well, I brush my teeth every morning." I say, "Cool. I don't want you to put this in your cupboard, because you'll forget about it. I want you to put it next to your toothbrush, so we don't have to build a new habit." Then I say, "Put a piece of tape on it with a 'two' on it, because I want you to take two of them." That's number one.

I'd go through that with each of the products, and then I'd say, "Okay, you can have all of these, you're good to go. Did you want to use the card you have on file?" Now they don't have to take their wallet out, and they don't have to make another purchase decision. They just have to say yes. It's a one-click upsell. It combines a prescriptive close with ghost products that set up trust. You're also being a good coach by showing them how they're going to make this happen in their routine.

This is obviously a consumption-based product, but for any product you want someone to use, they have to use it at a certain point. So we want to find ways to associate using the product with something they're already doing. Getting someone to form a new habit is incredibly difficult, and getting someone to break a habit is incredibly difficult, so we piggyback on habits they already have. That makes you both a better salesperson and a better coach if you're trying to help someone. That selling process, the setup, the close, getting the trust, and making the prescription, works unbelievably well.

If the person asks, "How much does this cost?" you can say, "Are you on a budget?" If they say yes, you say, "Okay, would you like me to order these by importance, with the things I think you absolutely need versus the things that will just get you more benefits faster?" Then I'd take one off and say, "This would be the lowest one I'd remove. Does that work for you?" If they say, "I can't do that," I'd say, "Really getting down to the bare bones here. I could take this one out, but here's what I'd want you to add to your diet to replace it." You don't want to show that what you took out isn't important, because then it looks like you're selling stuff that doesn't matter. These are ingredients, but you're going to have to eat a ton of broccoli. "Are you okay with that?" "You know what, I'll just get the thing." "Okay, cool." Now it's back in.

When you're selling something, you're trying to solve a problem. If they don't buy the thing, you reintroduce the problem you were solving: "I don't want you to have to eat a pound of broccoli, which is why I recommended this." They trust me to make the prescription, because that's why they're paying for my expertise.

Then you can pause and ask, "Are there things from your old identity that you're going to stop doing as a result of this program? Are you going to go out less? Drink less? Smoke less?" And I'd say, "Cool, how much of that do you do? We just found some money. Don't worry about it. Let's associate these with your new activities, your new identity." If this sounds really smooth, it's because I've done it a lot of times. You'll get used to it.

One of the best gifts you can give yourself is to tack yourself onto the existing sales process of a bigger business. If you work at a business that sells lots of low-ticket items, think car washes, massages, nails, hair, places that see lots of people every day at low prices, see if you can work with the business owner and inject yourself into their sales process. That way you get rep after rep after rep, and it will teach you more about the skill of selling and dealing with people than any course ever will. It will equip you for whatever you want to sell for the rest of your life. One of the best things I ever did was complete tons and tons of low-ticket transactions, so that when I got to selling higher-ticket, more expensive things, I thought, oh my God, this is a breeze.

This works for services too. Let's say you sell web design services and you have a laundry list of things you could offer. You can say, "We can do this, but it's not really our core capability, and it would probably cost you more if we did it, because we'd have to allocate more resources. There are guys in the Philippines who will do it for you. I've got a connection, and I can talk to you about it after this call."

And if you really want to be sneaky about it, and this is harder if you're a salesperson, so owners should tell their salespeople this, you can make it seem like the salesperson is leaning to the customer's side of the table: "Hey, man, you don't need to worry about this. I'll hook you up with the number of a guy." Then the salesperson becomes incredibly trusted, as if they're on the customer's side. What the customer doesn't know is that they're trying to sell the other stuff at the end of the day.

The goal as a salesperson is to move to their side of the table. If you start the conversation across the table, the goal is to end up shoulder to shoulder, so you're both looking at the decision together, with the same information, to make the best decision for the person. If you can walk yourself around the table psychologically, this is one of the fastest ways to do it.

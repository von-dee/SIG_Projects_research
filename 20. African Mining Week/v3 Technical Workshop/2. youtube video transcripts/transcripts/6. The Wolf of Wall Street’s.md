## Chapter 6: The Power of Tonality

How do you, in a period of four seconds, come across as sharp, enthusiastic, and an expert all at once? You can't say, "Hey, Bill, I'm sharp as a tack. I'm an expert." People will laugh at you. The truth is, the right words don't exist. The wrong words exist. You could say, "Hi, I'm a, uh... nice to meet you." That's not going to go very well. So there are certain words you'll say, and I'll hand them to you on a silver platter. They're very simple and basic. The magic isn't in the words. The magic is in *how* you say the words, meaning the tonalities you use when you start speaking.

When you're in person, it's body language and tonality combined. Watch what we have. Words make up about 9% of human communication, 10% more or less. Tonality is 45%, and body language is 45%. When you're on the telephone, body language vanishes because they can't see you, but it collapses into tonality, and they'll actually make a mental image of how they think you look based on how you sound.

So that magic way of getting someone to perceive you the right way, so you can take control, isn't really about the words you say. Words create what's called conscious communication. You focus on someone's words, you hear them, and you try to work out their logical meaning. That's important, so you can't say stupid things. One of the rules of building rapport and having charisma is not saying stupid things. That's a separate issue. But far more important, from the perspective of being perceived as an expert who's sharp and enthusiastic, is your tonality.

There are actually **ten core tonalities** we use with the straight line system, and I'll go through them very quickly toward the end of this session so you own them. There's a lot more to learn, because they're so powerful. A tonality is like the secret language of influence. When you use tonality correctly and say certain words, the person will actually hear additional words you haven't said.

For example, say I tell you, "Hi, this is Jordan Belfort calling from XYZ Company in New York. How are you today?" They're like, "Oh, Jesus. Another salesperson." They know you're a salesperson, and they hate your guts. They're yelling at their assistant, "How'd you let this person through the screen?" I would never do that, but that's what most cold callers do.

Watch. That's called **declaratives**: declarations, sentences. "Hi, I'm Jordan Belfort, calling from XYZ Company in New York." Three declarations. Then "How are you today?" That means "You know that I know that you know that I don't give a damn how you're doing today. It's just a perfunctory greeting that human beings give." I would never do that.

Here's what I do instead. I say, "Hey, it's, uh, Jordan Belfort? Calling from XYZ Company? In New York? How you doing today?" Those are called **phrasing declaratives as questions**. "Hey, it's Jordan Belfort?" They're thinking, "Who? Do I know a Jordan?" Suddenly they're asking themselves, "Do I know Jordan Belfort?" and they start searching their memory, because you asked it as a question. "From XYZ Company?" They think, "Do I know XYZ Company in New York?" While their brain starts to spin, trying to figure out whether they know this person, you say, "How you doing today?" with a really nice, upbeat greeting, so they think, "Wow, he really wants to know." It's not the stupid "how you doing today." It's *how* you doing today?

By layering on these tonalities, you almost put their brain into search mode. It's like when you've maxed out the RAM on a computer and you get that spinning wheel of death until the computer sorts it out. It's exactly what happens in a person's mind. The conscious mind has very little processing power and can only understand so much. The unconscious mind has unlimited processing power. By putting on these tonalities very early, what they hear is: "Hey, it's Jordan Belfort, you know me, right?" (unspoken words) "Calling from XYZ Company, you've heard of us, right? In New York, yeah, you know. How you doing today? I really want to know." I don't say the words "I really want to know," but they're trying to process all that information.

So instead of narrating against you in their internal monologue, saying, "Oh my God, why the hell is this guy calling me?", they say, "Uh, um, yeah, I'm doing good." You say, "Great. Now, if you recall," and I lower my voice, put a little mystery and intrigue in there, "you reached out to our company about three weeks ago, sent in a card requesting information on whatever it was. Does that ring a bell?" And they're like, "Oh, yeah, I remember." They think, "Damn, this person is sharp."

I use that and go right into my intelligence gathering. I start asking a series of very smart questions to identify their needs, values, pain points, and hierarchy of values, all the things I need to know, including financial qualification if that matters. And I am in complete control.

So now I've taken you down the straight line a bit. **Step one**, I took immediate control of the conversation. **Step two**, I used that control to gather the intelligence I need to see whether the person is even right for what I'm selling. If they're not right, I don't want to waste twenty minutes on the phone or in person with them. I want to get out of that sale as quickly as possible. Time is money. I've also been able to establish everything I need to know to tailor my presentation to what they actually need. It's not one-size-fits-all.

And by using active listening and asking smart questions, I'm getting into very tight rapport with them. So by the time I've asked everything I need to ask, I know whether I can help them, whether my product is right, and whether they can afford it. If the answer is yes, I'll say, "Well, Jim, Jill, John, based on everything you just said to me, this product is a perfect fit for you. Let me tell you why." And they're hanging on my every word. That's called my **straight line transition**, which is **step three**. "Based on everything you said to me, this program, this service, whatever it might be, is a perfect fit for you." Not "based on everything you said to me, it's a no." *Based on everything you said to me, it's a perfect fit for you. Let me tell you why.* I lower my voice, with scarcity, using tonality on every word. There's always a tonality. And from there, I've executed my straight line transition.

Now, if the person is not qualified, if based on the intelligence I gathered I can't help them and they're not right for the product, I'll say, "Listen, honestly, Jim, based on what you said, this is not really a great fit for you. I think maybe you should go elsewhere." You can send them elsewhere where they can get help, and you should if you can, but you don't want to waste your time making a presentation. You get it?

What happens next is that we use this to transition to the next step, which is actually creating certainty. We're going to sell them now, creating certainty that our product, our solution, our service, is the best in class and perfect for them. ## Chapter 7: The Three Tens of Sales

What is a straight line presentation? This gets back to the three things that must line up in every sale. Let's go through them.

When you think about sales, what is sales? At the highest level, **sales is the transference of emotion**. The primary emotion you're transferring is certainty. Someone has to be absolutely certain. And when we think about that, it must be the product or the service. We'll say "product" because it's easier, but it could be either. They have to be certain that the product is a perfect fit, that it will resolve their problem, eliminate their pain, and fill their needs. It's priced correctly, it's a great value proposition, and it has a great cost-benefit ratio, all those things we think about. They must be absolutely certain your product is that, or else why would they buy? If they think there's a better solution out there, you're the wrong solution, and they're not going to buy. That makes sense, right? So when we think about certainty in sales, we think about the product. They must be certain it's awesome, the best in its class, and that it's going to solve their problem and eliminate their pain.

I want you to imagine a continuum of certainty, a line from one to ten. A ten means they're in a state of absolute certainty. They think your product is the best thing in the world, the best thing since sliced bread. A one means they think it's the biggest piece of junk ever. They hate it and would never buy it. The question: where do you want someone on this scale when you ask them for the order?

That's going to happen after you make your presentation. You'll ask for the order for the first time. You make the presentation, then transition into your close, and tell them exactly, in no uncertain terms: "Here's what I suggest you do. Here's what you need to do. Get started with X." You lay it out very clearly. You tell them the price. You say, "Believe me, you're going to be very, very impressed. Sound good?" And you ask for the order directly. It's the same way a doctor says, "Here's what you need to do." He doesn't leave you without a prescription. You give them the prescription, you explain it, then you ask them to buy. That ends what's called the **front half** of the sale.

So again, we think about the certainty you're transferring. Where do you want them? Close to a ten or a one? Obviously a ten. Of course there's a five in the middle, where they're neither certain nor uncertain. A seven is more certain but not as certain as a ten. A three is less certain than a five but not as bad as a one. The point is that you're trying to get this person incredibly certain about the product, and you do that through your presentation. You're making an airtight, logical case. We start with logic about why this is awesome, with no "just trust me, buy." Logically, you lay out the case, the value proposition, and the cost-benefit ratio. You explain in no uncertain terms how it resolves their problems and eliminates their pain.

Let's say you do that effectively. You spend whatever time it takes to lay out the value, and it won't be that long the first time you ask for the order. When you're done, they're in a state of absolute certainty. They're a nine or a ten. The question: will they buy from you, yes or no? The answer is not yes, and it's not no. The answer is **maybe**. Maybe they will, maybe they won't.

Why would that be, if they're absolutely certain? What if, in the process of making them certain, with the things you said, the demonstrations, the sales aids, how you explained yourself, how you asked your intelligence-gathering questions, everything you did, the way you did it somehow made them not trust you or not like you? Will they buy from you? No. They don't trust you, but they love the product. They're not going to buy from you. They'll find someone else they trust, find the same or a similar product, and buy from them. So it's not enough that they love the product. They also must trust and connect with you, the salesperson.

So the first ten, and we call these the **three tens**, is the product. They must love the product. Two, they must trust and connect with you. If those two line up, will they buy now? Again, the answer is not yes, not no. It's maybe, because there's a third ten: they must trust the company that stands behind the product. They might love the product. They might think you're a great, trustworthy person. But they've heard things about your company. It gives terrible customer service, or it might be doing something fraudulent. It's not a trustworthy company. Good luck trying to close that person.

Essentially, as you move someone down the straight line from the open to the close, you're taking these steps. **Step one**, you take control of the sale by establishing those three crucial things: sharp, enthusiastic, expert. **Step two**, you use that to gather intelligence and build rapport. **Step three**, you execute your transition if they're right for the product. **Step four**, you make your initial straight line presentation to build massive certainty for the three tens, especially the first ten, and a little bit for yourself as well. If you can lace some of that in about yourself, through the way you carry yourself, then when you transition into asking for the order for the first time, and they're close enough on all three, you have an excellent shot at closing them. Sometimes people even enter already pretty certain, because they knew about your product beforehand. But if one of those three tens is deficient, below a five, you've got no shot at all. That's the front half of the straight line.

## Chapter 8: Handling Objections

So what happens when someone is uncertain about one of the three tens? You ask them for the order. You say, "Believe me, you're going to be very impressed. Sound good? Sound fair enough?" Do they say, "Nah, I don't like your product. I'm not certain enough. I need to be made more certain. I don't trust you"? No. "I don't trust you" is fighting words. People don't say that. People don't really get honest. What they do is dissemble. They say, "Oh yeah, it sounds good. Let me think about it. Let me call you back. Let me check with my wife. Send me some information. Bad time of year. I'm not liquid right now." In other words, they hit you with objections.

**Objections are smoke screens for uncertainty.** When people are uncertain, rather than telling you flat out, which is very in-your-face, "I don't trust you" or "I don't like your product," they hit you with objections. It would be great if someone just came out and said, "I don't trust you." I'd say, "Well, great. Let me tell you why you're wrong, why you should." I'd say things that increased their level of trust in me. But they don't say that. They hit you with objections.

Knowing that, we take common objections and use them as opportunities to increase the level of certainty for each of the three tens, and then seamlessly, smoothly, elegantly transition into another close.

You get hit with an objection. There's going to be a rebuttal to that objection. There always is, and there are standard rebuttals. But if you just rebut an objection, what happens? They'll just switch to a new objection, because you haven't solved the real problem. The objection was really a smoke screen. Someone says, "I want to think about it," and you just give them all the reasons why they shouldn't think about it: "I've been doing this a long time, people say they want to think about it and they never get started, I know you want to do this, blah blah blah." Whatever you say, you then ask for the order again, and they say, "Yeah, I understand that. Let me speak to my wife." They just hop to a new objection. Why? Because the underlying problem hasn't been addressed. The underlying problem is one of certainty. You have to increase certainty.

The way we do that is through a powerful technique called **straight line looping**, which is very easy to learn but a bit too long for this master class. I will tell you that looping is the art of taking a common objection and addressing it, but then, rather than trying to close, looping back into presentation mode and giving a secondary presentation that builds on top of the first.

Your initial presentation focuses mostly on the product or service, but it's framing. It's like framing a house. You're not building the whole house. You lay the foundation, put up the drywall, maybe put a roof on. There's no electric or plumbing yet. It's not a finished house. So when you get hit with an objection, you still have a lot of awesome things to say. You'll have a secondary pattern designed to increase certainty for the product, for you, and for the company. You want multiple language patterns for each of the three tens. So when you get hit with objections, you can answer the objection, but not just go ask for the order. You loop back into presentation mode, build certainty for each of the three tens, and then ask for the order again. By doing that, you very quickly get to the point where you can close anyone who's closable.

Those are four elements: the three tens, and then this other element called looping. Part of looping is also **lowering the action threshold**. You say things like, "Let me ask you an honest question. What's the worst that can possibly happen? Let's say I'm wrong. Let's say it's not the greatest thing in the world. Let's say it doesn't work for you." Most products have some sort of guarantee or rescission period, or the commitment is so small and the risk so small that it won't destroy their life. On the upside, and you tell them the upside, you **future pace** them. Imagine yourself six months from now. Everything is working perfectly. You're feeling great, you're feeling empowered, you're making more money. Just imagine that. What you're doing is making them run a positive movie, and telling them the negative isn't really that negative. That's called lowering someone's action threshold. That's your fourth element, and you do it with looping.

And lastly, the fifth element. There are five elements in all. It's called the **pain threshold**. As part of your intelligence gathering, remember I said you're going to identify pain. What's keeping them up at night? What's the worry that rests at the base of their skull and keeps them thinking? Once you know that, you don't want to resolve that pain yet, because at the end, when they're on the fence, you want to reintroduce that pain, remind them of it, and amplify it. What that does is lower their action threshold. It makes them more likely to buy, easier buyers. People are more prone to buy when they're feeling pain.

Let me give you a quick example. My father had what's called a very high action threshold. He was very tough to sell to. He didn't like salespeople. His belief was that his best decisions were made slowly and painfully. So my father was a very difficult person to close. He loved all his possessions, but of all things, he loved his car most. He would never let anybody touch his car except the local guy, Jimmy, at the Sunoco station. That was it. If Enzo Ferrari wanted to fix my dad's car, he'd say, "Enzo? Oh, Enzo, no, Jimmy's my guy." And no one else. That was my dad. No one was touching his car.

That's what high-action-threshold people are like. They're very difficult to sell. They actually have one person who sells them every type of product or service. They don't have lots of people. They have one. They're great customers once you have them.

One day, when I was about eleven, we were on a family vacation, driving from New York down to Florida. We were about eight hours out of New York, in a very bad part of, I think, Maryland, and suddenly the radiator started steaming and the car stalled on the side of the road about a mile from a gas station. The sun was setting. My father was beside himself, with his family there. What do you think he did? As much as he loved Jimmy at the Sunoco station, he walked to that gas station and said, "Fix my car. I don't care what it costs." Why? Because in that moment he was feeling massive pain. It was all over his face. So his action threshold dropped.

That's how you use pain to close sales. You remind someone, you reintroduce it. You ask, "What do you think is going to happen in another six months if you don't fix this problem now? How much worse will it get? How much is it going to cost you? How much will it disempower your company, your business, you personally?" Make them admit it. It lowers their action threshold and makes them easier to sell to.

Those are your **five core elements**.

## Chapter 9: Mastering the Ten Tonalities

On some level, the way to explain the straight line is that you're almost like a safecracker, but you're cracking the safe of the human mind. There are five numbers in the combination. The first number is the product: they must love the product. The second number: they must trust you. The third number: they have to trust the company behind the product. The fourth is lowering the action threshold. The fifth is the pain threshold.

When you're moving someone down the straight line, imagine a professional safecracker. What are they doing? They put their ear to the safe. They hear the first click. They spin it the other way: left 23. They hear a click. They go the other way: right 37. And they pull down. That's the equivalent of asking for the order. If it opens, you've closed. Happy days.

But very often it doesn't. So what does the safecracker do? He doesn't say, "Oh, the safe is uncrackable." He goes back to the first number and tries again. He always goes back to the first number, rebuilds, and hears the second. If he gets it on the second try, he closes on the second attempt. That's what you're doing. You're running these patterns. If you ask for the order and they don't buy, it just means you haven't fully figured out the combination. You might have the first three numbers correct, and you have to work on the last two. That's how it works.

I'll tell you one thing about the straight line: it has what's called a **good enough factor**. It's incredibly easy to learn, and even if you're just okay at it, you get incredible results. When you get great at it, you very quickly get to the point where you can close anyone who's closable. That's where I want you to be, and this master class is the first step in that process. Bottom line.

Now, real quick, I want to tell you about the ten tonalities.

1. **Scarcity.** Lowering your voice to a whisper, but with power behind it, signaling that something is in short supply. It's almost a secret.
2. **Certainty.** This is absolutely the best thing in the world. You're certain, tone and all.
3. **The reasonable man.** "Sound fair enough? I'm reasonable, you're reasonable."
4. **Phrasing the declarative as a question**, which I mentioned already. That's when you say, "Hey, Jordan Belfort? Calling from XYZ Company? In New York?"
5. **I care.** "How you doing today?" with real warmth, like you care.
6. **I really want to know.** "So what's that thing that's really keeping you up at night? I really want to know."
7. **Implied obviousness.** It's beyond obvious. "Yeah, yeah, you'll make money with this, but more importantly, what I can do for you besides this one transaction, over the long term. I want to be an asset to both you and your family."
8. **Sincerity.** Speaking with utter sincerity, which is different from certainty, where I'm saying this is the best thing. Sincerity is: "Believe me, you're going to be glad you did this."
9. **Money aside.** Where you say to someone, "Now let me ask you a question. Does the idea make sense to you? Do you like the idea?" Implicit in the way I said that is "money aside, hypothetically speaking," as opposed to "Well, let me ask you a question: does the idea make sense to you?", at which point they say, "What the hell? No, it doesn't." So that's your disarming, hypothetical money-aside tonality.
10. **Mystery and intrigue.** Where you're drawing them in. "Now, John, the reason for the call today..." It's similar to scarcity, but with more mystery. "Now, the reason for the call today."

# People don’t buy ideas. They buy you. | Robert Herjavec

[]()

[www.youtube.com/watch?v=etWQH-OHnwI](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=etWQH-OHnwI)



We had somebody come out on *Shark Tank* one time when I was in a grumpy mood. Why was I grumpy? Probably because I was hungry, and I wasn't really paying attention. This person was pitching their idea, and they cut me off and said, "Hey, you're not listening to me." I said, "Hang on a second. It's not my responsibility to listen. It's your responsibility to be heard."

If you ever watch  *Shark Tank* , sometimes people come out and don't know their numbers, and we're like, "Oh my gosh, how do you not know your numbers? How did you get out here? You should leave. That's a horrible idea." Then literally the next pitch, someone else doesn't know their numbers either, and we say, "That's okay. We can hire an accountant to help you." What's the difference? The person who did well sold themselves.

Bad pitches go right into the numbers and the features. But before you sell me your idea, take a little time to build a connection and sell yourself. I want to know about you. Not just how many kids you have or where you go for dinner, but what this idea means to you. I want to know if you're invested in it and how you came up with it. If you're selling an incredible efficiency idea to your company, tell me how you came up with it. Tell me what it means to you. Then tell me why you think it's going to work.

On  *Shark Tank* , the average pitch, believe it or not, runs over an hour, and that hour gets cut down to seven minutes. Here's an amazing fact, and all of us agree on it: 50% of the time, we know whether we're in or out within the first two minutes. The subconscious message is more powerful than the conscious mind.

What do I mean by that? Somebody comes out and they're standing stiffly, or they're all over the map. You know what that makes me? Nervous. When you're moving around like that, I'm subconsciously thinking, "Do I trust this person? They're frantic. What's going on?"

My point is that *you* have to equal the idea. If you're selling something about stability and security, you need to portray stability and security. I'm not necessarily talking about wearing a suit and tie, but you are the representative of your idea. If you're selling a fun idea to increase customer engagement, like a great event you think the company should run, you'd better be having fun. You'd better be smiling. Look at me, I'm smiling. I want to sell you a fun idea. But if you're pitching a new way to increase sales at a funeral parlor, you probably shouldn't be smiling. You have to be equal to the moment.

That's what we look for after 18 years: is the person who came out to pitch equal to what's about to come out of their mouth? It works the same way within a company.

The key to any idea or any communication is to speak the language of the person you're presenting to. The higher up you go in an organization, the more you talk about outcomes, value, and business propositions. If you're pitching your great idea to your manager, you should speak one way. But if you're pitching it to the CEO, you need to start thinking about business outcomes, finance, expenditure, and ROI, return on investment. Cater your idea to the person you're presenting to.

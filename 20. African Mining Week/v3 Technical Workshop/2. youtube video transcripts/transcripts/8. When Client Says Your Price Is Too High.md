# Narration: The $1,000 Video Negotiation

**Round one: the polite walk-away**

The call opens with a friendly greeting. Mo, who owns a retail store, tells Chris he's glad Chris could take the call. He's seen the "banger" videos on Chris's website and wants one made for his business. Chris says he'd love to do it and asks about budget. Mo answers that he has a thousand dollars.

Chris doesn't jump at it. He asks whether the video is really important to Mo, and Mo says it will be if Chris makes it. Chris says a thousand dollars doesn't solve much, so he asks what Mo actually wants to achieve. Mo explains that the goal is awareness, since nobody is walking into the store. Chris asks how much awareness. Mo says he'd like two to three more people walking in each day.

Chris turns that into money. Mo's average product sells for about $100, so two to three extra customers a day means roughly $200 to $300 in added daily revenue. They settle on $250 as the midpoint, and over a 30-day month that comes to about $7,500 in new revenue.

Chris then asks whether spending $1,000 to chase $7,500 a month feels proportionate. He points out it's more than ten percent, and asks whether Mo would spend a thousand dollars every month. Mo says maybe, if this first one goes well. Chris says he's concerned the budget is too small and quotes the business philosopher Jim Rohn. Mo admits that, put that way, it does feel disproportionate. Mo pushes back and asks whether Chris will guarantee $7,500 a month. Chris says he's not promising that, only trying to weigh effort against result.

Chris says that when someone tells him they don't have much money for a project, his first instinct is to skip it, because it signals the project isn't truly important. Mo agrees that would be his instinct too. Chris asks where else Mo could spend that thousand dollars to make a bigger impact, since he doesn't want to take money for something unimportant. Mo says the video is important, but not "a thousand dollars" important.

Chris asks Mo to compare it to his other spending: the refrigeration unit, the air conditioning, the polished floors, and whether any of them changed the business. He also mentions the BMW M5 parked outside and says that was clearly important to Mo. In his experience, people spend money on what they value, the way someone will pay more for a fancy pair of pants and otherwise buy the cheaper version.

Chris adds a warning. Businesses often see competitors make videos and develop "video envy," then pay for an ad just to keep up with the Joneses, which he doesn't think is a good use of money. Doing the job properly would take a long conversation about purpose, goals, and targeting, and that conversation would be out of proportion to a thousand-dollar problem. He asks what Mo wants to do. Mo says he won't spend more than $1,000. Chris says then he shouldn't, that Chris isn't the person for it, and that he'd discourage Mo from spending that money with anyone.

Mo says he'll probably look for someone who will do it for a thousand. Chris says that's his prerogative. Mo asks if Chris can recommend anyone, and Chris suggests Fiverr, where he's heard they can do it for five bucks. They part politely, with Chris wishing him luck.

**The debrief: talking about money**

The scene shifts to a group discussion about what just happened. One participant calls it a very advanced conversation about how to talk about money. Many people starting out are so hungry for a thousand-dollar job, when they're used to $200 or $500 jobs, that they never have this kind of conversation. They take the job anyway, then spend hours on revisions and never reach what the client really needs.

Another participant asks, half in challenge, what to do if you can't say no and have to take the job. The others say they're not claiming you always can. They want people online to understand the dynamics. They share a story about someone who found a videographer charging $80 and was told to hire him. Their own minimum for a video is $1,000, and a typical project runs around six grand. Spend your eighty dollars and you get what you pay for, they say, but they can't help with that.

They acknowledge that for some people turning down a thousand dollars is hard, because it's a lot of money. The lesson from their pro group is that you need a financial runway to say no, and that you should ask educated questions. Someone points out that you didn't have the thousand dollars before the call came in, and you were going to make life work anyway. Don't let one call push you into a disadvantaged position where you feel you must close it.

**Round two: you can't say no**

They decide to replay the scene with new rules. This time Chris cannot turn the client away or refer him to Fiverr, and Mo's budget is a firm $1,000. They skip the opening and pick up at the budget conversation. Mo says he really, really doesn't want to spend more than a thousand dollars.

Chris says he understands that a thousand is a lot for Mo, and he proposes an alternative. He'll make the video for free, and if it delivers the results Mo wants, he'll take a percentage of the results. There's no risk to Mo. Mo says that sounds fantastic and asks what percentage Chris has in mind. Chris says the difference in traffic and revenue is hard to measure, so he'd rather be paid per new customer who walks in. Since each customer spends about $100, he suggests half: $50 per customer. He takes all the risk and relies on Mo's honesty.

Mo hesitates. Chris asks what's holding him back. Mo says he worries Chris will make a banger of a video, it will pull in lots of customers, and then he'll be out half that revenue. Chris says banger videos are all he makes, and Mo agrees, saying that's why he called in the first place. Mo asks what happens if the payout exceeds a thousand dollars. Chris says that's exactly the point, and he's the one taking the risk. He quotes Peter Drucker, saying that all profit comes from risk. His offer is simple: he takes the risk, or Mo pays him what he's worth and keeps all the profit. Mo asks what he's worth, and Chris says $4,000, his minimum.

Mo needs a moment to think. He asks what a banger video actually includes, then asks whether they're really entertaining a $4,000 budget. He says he'd want a cap on the percentage Chris could earn, because if the video brings in a flood of customers, he doesn't want Chris banking $10,000 for a single video that may not cost that much to make. Chris asks whether Mo is saying he doesn't want a long line of customers. Mo says he does, but he doesn't want to pay forever. Chris points out that he just set a $4,000 minimum and asks whether Mo thinks he'd be paid more than he needs.

Chris lays out the two options on the table. In the first, Mo pays $4,000 upfront and keeps every dollar after that. In the second, Chris does it free and takes 50 percent of all new net revenue with no limit. When Mo balks, Chris offers to limit the revenue share to the first year. He tells Mo he's a wise businessman and can see him running the numbers, picturing how much money the video will bring in and how much he'll pay "this punk kid." Chris suggests that paying the $4,000 is probably the more prudent choice, but it's Mo's call.

Mo says he's impressed by the back and forth and Chris's confidence in his work. He believes the video will bring in more than $4,000 in value, so he'll go with the four thousand. Chris agrees and says he'll send a proposal. They settle on $2,000 upfront to get started.

**The closing commentary**

Coming back to the group, the participants highlight one more lesson for people just starting out: everything is a negotiation. A client offering $1,000 doesn't mean you must deliver everything they imagine. If they want a one-minute video, you can offer a 30-second one for a thousand. If they don't meet you at your price, you don't have to meet them at their deliverable. Don't be afraid to change the scope of the work to something you consider fair.

The host loves that the pro group is so pro that they're doing meta commentary on top of the meta commentary. The others say they're just looking out for people starting out, putting themselves on the spot so everything gets explained. The host compares it to watching a movie with the director's commentary running over it as the movie is being made, and says that's how wild this exercise is.

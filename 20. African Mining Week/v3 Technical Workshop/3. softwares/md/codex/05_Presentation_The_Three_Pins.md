# Presentation 1: The Three Pins

## The field-season decision

**Session:** African Mining Week, Technical Track  
**Panel theme:** The Intelligent Mine: Connecting AI Across the Full Value Chain, From First Drill Hole to Final Rehabilitation  
**Format:** Solo presentation, 30 minutes, visual aid, no Q&A  
**Story lens:** An exploration team has one field season and three candidate targets. Follow the moment when the team decides where to spend its next unit of effort.

> **Scenario note:** This is a fictional composite for presentation. The date, place, characters, mine, dialogue, and events are illustrative, not a report of a real project or result.

## Story spine

Three candidate areas appear on a district map. The team has limited field capacity. MineSentinel establishes the landscape; MineAsk structures the site assessment; TerraPulse helps rank geological evidence; MyMine gives the operating team a shared site view as the project moves forward. Each step narrows uncertainty, but human review and verified data remain essential.

**Central promise:** AI earns its place when it helps the right people make a better connected decision, and shows its evidence and limits.

## Speaker walkthrough

### 0:00-3:00 — Open with the moment, not the market map

**Visual:** A Ghanaian district map. Three pins: North Ridge, River Bend, and East Line. A calendar shows the field window closing. The names are invented.

**Say:**

> “In this fictional scenario, it is 6:40 on a Monday morning at a small exploration office in Ghana. Ama opens the district map. Three pins. One field season. The operations lead looks over and asks, ‘Which one are we sending the team to first?’
>
> Ama has a problem. The geology is in one file. The concession notes are in another. The access questions are in an email thread. And the budget meeting starts at nine.
>
> She thinks, ‘If I choose from the cleanest spreadsheet instead of the strongest evidence, I’ll spend the season explaining the wrong choice.’
>
> That is the moment I want to stay inside. The mine does not need another isolated AI tool. It needs a connected path from a question to evidence, review, action, and the record of what happened next.”

Pause after the quoted question. Let the map remain on screen. Then move to the first product.

**Why this opening works:** It names a place and time, starts with action, gives the audience direct speech and an in-the-moment thought, and opens a question that the walkthrough will answer. The scenario is explicitly fictional rather than presented as a real customer story.

### 3:00-7:00 — MineSentinel: orient the team

**Visual:** Zoom from regional context to the three candidate locations. Show the mining map, concession visibility, and available production context. Only show real fields from the current build.

**Say:**

> “Ama starts with MineSentinel. First she sees the landscape: where the known mining activity sits, what concession context is visible, and what production information is available. She is not asking the map to decide where gold is. She is asking it to help the team see where the question lives.”

**Walkthrough:**

1. Start on the regional mining map.
2. Search or navigate to the fictional district.
3. Turn on available mine and concession layers.
4. Inspect candidate locations and nearby context.
5. Open available production analytics for sector context.
6. Mark what is relevant and what needs a source check.

**Expected result:** A shared landscape view and a shorter list of questions for site-level due diligence.

**Benefit:** Less manual map assembly; faster orientation across roles; a common starting point for the next assessment.

**Trust cue:** “Coverage, source authority, and data date matter. MineSentinel gives a screening view; Ama still checks official records before treating a boundary or production value as decisive.”

**But / therefore bridge:** The map gives Ama context, **but** it cannot answer every site-specific question. **Therefore**, she asks MineAsk to organize the evidence around the location.

### 7:00-12:00 — MineAsk: separate what is known from what is assumed

**Visual:** One candidate boundary on a map. Beside it, three columns: **Evidence / Interpretation / Verify**.

**Say:**

> “Ama selects River Bend. She asks a narrow question: ‘What should we verify about title, access, water, and nearby land constraints before fieldwork?’
>
> MineAsk brings available evidence into one assessment. Now the team can see which statements are sourced, which are an interpretation, and which remain a field or official check.”

**Walkthrough:**

1. Define the site by coordinates, concession identifier, or supported boundary input.
2. Select available cadastre, geology, terrain, water, road, settlement, protected-area, or satellite context.
3. Ask one narrow location-specific question.
4. Open the supporting evidence for any consequential response.
5. Add open checks to the **Verify** column and assign owners.
6. Produce or share a first-pass dossier only if that function is available in the build.

**Expected result:** A structured assessment, visible evidence, and named follow-up checks.

**Benefit:** Less repeated searching; earlier visibility into missing information; clearer handoff to geology, legal, environment, or field teams.

**Trust cue:** “MineAsk helps organize the review. It does not issue a permit, establish legal title, or replace counsel and field verification. Monitoring and alert features are described as partial or planned, so I will not present them as live unless confirmed.”

**Speech / pause:** “One red flag does not mean ‘stop.’ It means ‘look here.’” Pause, then open the evidence column.

**But / therefore bridge:** The site assessment makes the constraints visible, **but** the core exploration question is still geological. **Therefore**, Ama brings the strongest candidate evidence into TerraPulse.

### 12:00-18:00 — TerraPulse: rank evidence, not certainty

**Visual:** Survey layers appear one by one: satellite, geophysical, geochemical, structural. Candidate target areas emerge with an evidence matrix.

**Say:**

> “Ama loads the available survey evidence. She does not ask the software to promise a discovery. She asks it to make the evidence easier to compare, and to show where the next field check may be most useful.”

**Walkthrough:**

1. Open the project and state the exploration decision: where to validate next.
2. Inspect the supported data inventory, dates, coordinates, units, and coverage.
3. Compare relevant layers in the unified map.
4. Review the available prospectivity or anomaly ranking.
5. Open the evidence matrix to inspect supporting, conflicting, and missing information.
6. Have the geologist challenge the result and select a target for field validation.
7. Export target coordinates only if the format and geometry have been confirmed.

**Expected result:** A ranked shortlist with an explainable rationale and a field-validation plan.

**Benefit:** More focused survey and field effort; less manual data harmonization; better technical discussion before expensive work.

**Trust cue:** “Ranking is not a reserve statement, a resource estimate, or a guarantee of mineralization. TerraPulse supports geological judgment; it does not replace field validation or formal reporting.”

**Use the story dance:** The first layer raises a possibility. The next layer complicates it. A missing survey opens a question. The evidence matrix closes that loop with a testable action.

### 18:00-21:00 — Ama makes the call

**Visual:** The three pins return. Two are parked for now. One is selected for validation. Show an action card with reason, evidence, uncertainty, owner, and next check.

**Say:**

> “Ama looks at the ranking. One target is bright, but its access evidence is weak. Another is less dramatic, but multiple geological layers agree and the field route is understood.
>
> The team lead asks, ‘So we drill the bright one?’
>
> Ama answers, ‘Not yet. We validate the stronger case first. Then we decide what the next dollar earns us.’”

Explain that this is an illustrative line, not a quote from a real operator. Show how the decision should be recorded: evidence, uncertainty, reviewer, and next action.

**Expected result:** A human-owned decision with its reasoning preserved.

**Benefit:** The team can explain why it prioritized one target and what evidence would change its mind.

### 21:00-26:00 — MyMine: put the plan in one shared space

**Visual:** A shared mine twin. A planner on a tablet and a remote reviewer on web or headset, using only confirmed interfaces.

**Say:**

> “The project moves forward. Now the question is no longer only ‘where might we explore?’ It is ‘how does the team discuss the site and its plan together?’
>
> MyMine gives the group a shared AR mine model. The planner points to an access route. A remote reviewer sees the same location. The discussion starts from one spatial reference.”

**Walkthrough:**

1. Open the mine twin and state the model source and date.
2. Orient the room using a recognizable site landmark.
3. Invite reviewers on the supported devices.
4. Navigate to one planning question.
5. Show a live stream only if that source is actually integrated and timestamped.
6. Capture actions in the approved record system.

**Expected result:** On-site and remote users can review the same 3D context and leave with clearer actions.

**Benefit:** Less ambiguity about which plan or location the team is discussing; potentially better remote participation and meeting clarity.

**Trust cue:** “The source notes describe the twin, streaming, multi-user use, multiple device types, and edge sync as owner-stated capabilities. Accuracy, integrations, latency, offline duration, and advanced overlays still need validation. This is not a planning suite, SCADA, or certified safety system.”

### 26:00-29:00 — Connect the lifecycle without overclaiming

**Visual:** A value-chain line from opportunity to exploration, operation, logistics, ESG, and closure. Use solid boxes for the four product roles and dotted boxes for future integrations.

**Say:**

> “The wider intelligent-mine vision can add commodity forecasts, regulatory text monitoring, equipment telematics, remote sensing, traceability, plant optimization, logistics, and closure models. But those capabilities need data owners, integration, governance, and validation.
>
> The lesson is not that one application already does all of this. The lesson is that every new capability should connect to the next decision and leave a reviewable trail.”

Clarify that these broader capabilities come from the session brief and are not asserted as shipped functions of the four products.

### 29:00-30:00 — Close with a practical framework

**Visual:** One pilot card: **Decision / Data / Reviewer / Integration / Measure**.

**Say:**

> “When you evaluate the next AI investment, ask five things. Which decision changes? Which data supports it? Who reviews the output? What handoff does it connect? And how will we measure the result?
>
> Connect the evidence before you connect the machines.”

Stop after the closing line. The format has no Q&A.

## Presenter notes

- Start with the story's unresolved question, not the product list.
- Keep the fictional scenario clearly labeled. Do not imply the dialogue or mine is real.
- Lead with user benefit, then show the feature that delivers it.
- Earn technical trust with visible sources, dates, confidence, and limitations.
- Use a conversational voice; vary sentence length and pause after the key line.
- Rehearse the 30-minute clock. Trim optional layer detail rather than the human decision or close.

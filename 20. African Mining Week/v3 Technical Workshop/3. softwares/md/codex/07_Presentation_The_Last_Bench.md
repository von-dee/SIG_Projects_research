# Presentation 3: The Last Bench

## Start at closure. Walk back to the first decision.

**Session:** African Mining Week, Technical Track  
**Panel theme:** The Intelligent Mine: Connecting AI Across the Full Value Chain, From First Drill Hole to Final Rehabilitation  
**Format:** Solo presentation, 30 minutes, visual aid, no Q&A  
**Story lens:** Begin with a future mine closure review. A planner points at a rehabilitation map and asks where the evidence for today's choices will come from. Then walk backward through the operating review, the exploration target, the site assessment, and the original opportunity decision.

> **Scenario note:** This is a fictional composite set in a possible future. The date, location, people, dialogue, and rehabilitation scene are illustrative, not a real mine or forecast.

## Story spine

The story opens at the final bench of a fictional mine in 2040. The closure lead needs to understand what was disturbed, what was promised, and what evidence supports the plan. That question sends us backward: MyMine offers a shared spatial review; TerraPulse shows how target rationale can be preserved; MineAsk distinguishes sourced evidence from unresolved checks; MineSentinel gives the original landscape context. The arc then moves forward again with a practical framework for designing connected systems early.

**Central promise:** Closure readiness is shaped by decisions made from the first site screen onward. A connected evidence trail is a design choice, not a last-minute report.

## Speaker walkthrough

### 0:00-3:00 — Start with the ending

**Visual:** A quiet, restored mine bench in a fictional future. A closure lead holds a tablet with a site map. No dramatic claim or fabricated impact metric.

**Say:**

> “It is 2040 in this fictional scene. We are standing on the last bench of a mine that is moving into closure.
>
> The closure lead taps a map and asks, ‘Can we show why this area was disturbed, what we committed to do, and what evidence we used along the way?’
>
> The room goes quiet. The rehabilitation plan is here. The harder question is whether the decisions that came before it left a usable trail.
>
> I want to walk backward from this bench, then forward again. Because the quality of closure evidence is affected by choices made much earlier: where we looked, what we verified, and how the operating team shared its picture of the site.”

Pause after “the room goes quiet.” Clarify that the scene is illustrative. The opening starts at the ending, then creates a loop the presentation will close.

### 3:00-8:00 — MyMine: what can the site team see together?

**Visual:** The closure map transitions to a 3D mine twin. Mark old and current data with visible dates; do not imply the product has a closure module.

**Say:**

> “The first step backward is the operating mine. What did the planning team actually see when it made decisions about the site?
>
> MyMine is described as a shared AR mine twin, with streaming and multi-user sessions across device types. In a review, a planner and a remote specialist can point to the same place and discuss the same model.”

**Steps:**

1. Open the available mine twin and state its model source and date.
2. Orient the reviewers using a recognizable site landmark.
3. Invite colleagues on supported devices into the shared view.
4. Navigate to one historical or current planning question, using only available data.
5. If a live stream is demonstrated, show its source and timestamp.
6. Record the decision and link to the source system that owns the formal record.

**Expected result:** A shared visual review that can improve common understanding of the site context.

**Benefit:** Less ambiguity across disciplines and more useful participation from remote specialists.

**Trust cue:** “MyMine's notes describe the twin, collaboration, streaming, multi-device access, and edge sync as owner-stated. Accuracy, latency, integrations, and offline resilience must be tested. It is not a closure-accounting system unless such a capability is independently confirmed.”

**But / therefore bridge:** A shared model helps the team discuss the site, **but** it cannot explain why the original target was selected. **Therefore**, we go further back to TerraPulse.

### 8:00-13:00 — TerraPulse: preserve why the team looked there

**Visual:** Rewind through a timeline to exploration. Show satellite, geophysical, geochemical, and structural layers with a target rationale.

**Say:**

> “Before the mine plan, there was an exploration question. Why this target? What did the team see, and what did it still need to test?”

**Steps:**

1. Open the exploration project's saved boundary and decision question.
2. Review the supported data inputs, dates, coordinate systems, and coverage.
3. Compare relevant layers in the unified map.
4. Review a target ranking and its evidence matrix.
5. Read the counter-evidence and missing-data notes.
6. Open the field-validation rationale or target export if available.
7. Explain how field results should be returned to update the interpretation.

**Expected result:** A technical rationale linking target selection to evidence and follow-up work.

**Benefit:** Makes the basis of exploration prioritization easier to revisit and can reduce time spent reconstructing why a target was advanced.

**Trust cue:** “A ranked target is not a discovery, a resource estimate, or a reserve. TerraPulse supports geologists; field validation and formal reporting remain essential.”

**Bridge:** The target rationale is clearer, **but** it also depends on whether the location and its constraints were understood. **Therefore**, we inspect the original site assessment in MineAsk.

### 13:00-18:00 — MineAsk: keep the original uncertainty attached

**Visual:** A site assessment timeline, with each item labeled by source date. Columns read **Known / Interpreted / Verified later**.

**Say:**

> “The closure question sends us into the first assessment. What did the team know about title, water, access, nearby land uses, and regulatory steps? What was still uncertain?”

**Steps:**

1. Open the assessment for the fictional concession or location.
2. Inspect the site boundary and selected mining/geospatial context layers.
3. Review the original question and structured response.
4. Open evidence sources and compare their dates with later verification.
5. Separate sourced claims, system interpretation, and checks completed by people.
6. Identify missing items and assign responsibility to the right human reviewer.
7. Show a dossier or version history only if that capability exists in the current build.

**Expected result:** A first-pass assessment where the evidence and unresolved questions are visible and attributable.

**Benefit:** Reduces repeated research and helps prevent an early assumption from becoming an invisible fact in later decisions.

**Trust cue:** “MineAsk is a query-and-assess aid. It does not guarantee compliance, legal title, or real-time regulatory monitoring. Some monitoring and reporting features are partial or planned.”

### 18:00-22:00 — MineSentinel: return to the original landscape

**Visual:** Zoom out to the regional map that originally framed the opportunity. Show only available map, production, concession, and GIS context.

**Say:**

> “One more step backward. Before the site became an exploration project, it was a pin on a regional map. What did the team know about the wider mining landscape when it began?”

**Steps:**

1. Open MineSentinel's mining map and locate the region.
2. Inspect available nearby mine and concession context.
3. Review available production analytics and identify the period they cover.
4. Open relevant GIS context if the data is present.
5. Note source, date, missing coverage, and the original screening question.
6. Export a briefing only if the deployed product supports it and the output is reviewed.

**Expected result:** A regional baseline that explains where the opportunity entered the team's decision process.

**Benefit:** Less time reconstructing basic sector context and more consistent analysis across projects.

**Trust cue:** “The map and analytics establish context. They do not prove legal status, complete sector coverage, or future production.”

### 22:00-26:00 — Walk forward and show the connected lifecycle

**Visual:** Reverse the timeline. The four products appear in order: MineSentinel → MineAsk → TerraPulse → MyMine. Extend dotted lines to operations, ESG, and closure.

**Say:**

> “Now we move forward. MineSentinel locates the opportunity. MineAsk organizes the evidence around it. TerraPulse helps focus geological follow-up. MyMine gives teams a shared spatial view for site-level review.
>
> The session vision extends further: regulatory NLP, public-sentiment analysis, telematics, satellite and drone monitoring, traceability, real-time plant optimization, logistics, ESG sensing, and closure modeling. Those capabilities require verified integrations and accountable data owners. The dotted line means a connection to build and test, not a feature I am claiming is already live.”

**Expected result:** The audience sees the sequence and the points where integration and governance are needed.

**Benefit:** Moves the investment discussion from isolated departmental tools toward end-to-end decisions and handoffs.

### 26:00-29:00 — Evaluate the next AI investment clearly

**Visual:** A simple line: **Need → Evidence → Fit → Trust → Pilot**.

**Say:**

> “A technical audience should not have to choose between enthusiasm and skepticism. We can be clear about both. Start with the job to be done. Gather the operational facts. Show the feature only after the audience understands the benefit. Then establish trust: do we trust the output, the people presenting it, and the organization that will support it?
>
> If any one of those is weak, the answer is not a harder pitch. It is a smaller, better-defined pilot.”

**Pilot checklist:**

1. Name the decision and current workflow.
2. Baseline time, rework, and quality before launch.
3. Confirm data sources, dates, security, and owners.
4. Assign human reviewers and define what the system is not allowed to decide.
5. Agree on measurable exit criteria before the pilot begins.

### 29:00-30:00 — Close at the bench

**Visual:** Return to the future closure scene. The map now has source and date references visible, but no claim that the software created a closure plan.

**Say:**

> “We began at the last bench with one question: can we show why we made these choices? The answer starts much earlier than closure. It starts with the first map, the first evidence check, and the first decision that leaves a trail.
>
> Design the evidence trail before you need to defend it.”

Pause. End the solo address; do not invite Q&A.

## Presenter notes

- This is the rarest lens of the three: closure looking backward through the chain of decisions.
- Start with the ending, then work backward and return to the same image at the close.
- Use the same fictional project throughout; label the future scene and all dialogue as illustrative.
- Keep uncertainty visible. The purpose is to build justified confidence, not to push the audience to a predetermined conclusion.
- Solid lines mean described product scope; dotted lines mean integration opportunities from the session brief.
- Rehearse pauses after the closure lead's question and the final line.

# The First Capital Decision


[0:00-3:00]


**[Visual: Africa map. Three folders labeled Ghana, Burkina Faso, South Africa. Add “other candidate countries” as a fourth option. Do not highlight a winner.]**

**Say:**

> Good morning. I want to begin with one decision, not with a list of technologies.
>
> It is 8:15 on a Monday morning in this fictional scenario. Mariam, an investment director, moves three country folders across a table: Ghana, Burkina Faso, and South Africa. Her technical director looks at the field-season calendar and asks, “Which one gets the first exploration budget?”
>
> Mariam opens the spreadsheets. One uses a different reporting period. Another has a map at a different scale. The production definitions do not line up. And the licensing notes are in a separate document.
>
> She thinks, “If we compare unlike evidence, the neatest table may beat the strongest opportunity.”
>
> Notice what she is not asking the software to do. She is not asking it to declare a country the winner, grant a permit, discover gold, and run a mine. She needs a connected sequence of decisions: first the country and region, then the location, then the geological target, and finally a shared view for the field team.
>
> That is the journey I will walk through today. Each tool has a specific job. Each output becomes an input to the next decision. And at every step, a person remains responsible for checking the evidence.

**[Pause. Show the four words one at a time: Country. Location. Target. Field.]**

**[Visual/demo: Open MineSentinel's map. Use country coverage and data actually present. Show map, available concession context, production analytics, GIS view, and an export only if those functions are available in the current build.]**

**Say:**

> Mariam starts with MineSentinel. Her question is: “Which countries or regions deserve deeper investigation for this gold-mine opportunity?”
>
> Before opening a layer, the team agrees on the comparison criteria. They want to understand mining context, available production information, concession visibility, location patterns, and geographic evidence. They also bring their own commercial and risk criteria. The software can organize evidence; it cannot decide what matters most to this investor without those criteria.

**[Demonstrate the first screen.]**

> First, we open the interactive mining map. We begin at the broadest view and check which countries and records are represented. Here, I am using Ghana, Burkina Faso, and South Africa as candidate examples. I am not saying this screen proves one is better. We need comparable coverage and comparable dates before making that claim.
>
> Now we move from the country to the region. We turn on the available mine and concession layers and locate the areas the team wants to examine. The point of this map is orientation: where are known assets, where is the question, and what should we investigate next?

**[If production analytics are available, select the commodity and period. Show the labels, dates, and units.]**

> Next, we look at the production analytics that are available. We check the time period, units, and definitions. If the periods do not match, we say so. If a country has missing data, we do not fill the gap with a confident color.
>
> Production context can help the team frame a comparison. It is not a price forecast. It is not a reserve estimate. And it does not tell us what a new mine will produce.

**[If GIS layers are connected, open one, show its date/source. If not, use a labeled concept slide and state that the feed must be integrated.]**

> We can also inspect GIS intelligence where a relevant data feed exists. We check its source and date. A satellite or telemetry layer is useful only if we understand what it covers and how current it is.
>
> Finally, we create the screening brief. We include the criteria, the comparable information, the dates, and the questions that remain. Mariam's team uses this to choose a region for deeper due diligence—not to announce an automatic winner.

**[Show the shortlist artifact: “Advance for assessment / Hold for more evidence / Do not advance.” Use illustrative labels, not fabricated scores.]**

> The expected result is a shortlist that people can inspect. The benefit is less repeated manual map and data compilation, a faster first view of the landscape, and a clearer explanation of why a region advanced.
>
> MineSentinel's product notes describe mapping, production analytics, concession visibility, GIS dashboards, and report exports. They do not establish complete or equally current coverage across every African country. So before a real country comparison, we verify the data coverage, source authority, and update dates.
>
> Mariam now has a region worth checking. But a region is not a mine site. Therefore, she moves to the exact location and asks what needs to be verified before the team commits.



[9:00-16:00]


**[Visual/demo: Pick one fictional location from the shortlist. Show its boundary, evidence layers, a focused query, and three columns: Sourced / Interpreted / Verify.]**

**Say:**

> Mariam selects one location to investigate. Her question is: “What does the available evidence say about this site, and what must legal, environmental, and field specialists confirm?”
>
> First, we define the site. Depending on what the deployed version supports, that could be a concession identifier, coordinates, a map pin, a district, a route, a community, or an uploaded boundary. Then we check the geometry. If the boundary is wrong, every layer after this could mislead us.

**[Demonstrate selection and geometry check.]**

> Now we bring together the relevant context that is available: title or cadastre, geology, terrain, water, roads, infrastructure, settlements, protected areas, satellite context, weather, or market information.
>
> We don't turn on every layer just because it exists. We choose the evidence that matters to the decision. We compare the site with its surroundings. We look for overlaps, access dependencies, environmental questions, missing data, and conflicts between sources.

**[Demonstrate one focused question.]**

> Here is the question: “What licensing and access issues should we verify before field mobilization?”
>
> MineAsk helps organize a first-pass response. Now I want to show you the most important part: not only the answer, but how we inspect the answer.

**[Open an evidence card or source. If no citations are available in the build, do not simulate them; explain the review step conceptually.]**

> This column is sourced information. This column is interpretation. And this column is what we still need to verify.
>
> A map overlay may show an apparent boundary relationship. That is a question for the official registry. A regulatory pathway summary may help make a checklist. It is not legal advice and it is not an approval. A water or land layer may signal an issue to investigate. It does not replace an environmental assessment or community engagement.

**[Review scorecard factors if present. Open each factor; do not show a single score alone.]**

> If we use a screening or scorecard view, we inspect the factors one by one: geological context, legal status, access, ESG, operations. A combined rating is not enough. We need to know which evidence supports it, what is missing, and which issue could change the decision.

**[Show a regulatory checklist.]**

> The team now turns the available regulatory context into a checklist. For Ghana, we would confirm current rules and licensing steps with official sources and local counsel. We would do the same for whichever jurisdiction we select. The software helps us organize the questions; official sources and qualified people confirm the pathway.
>
> We also examine logistics and location context: roads, routes, ports, power, water, processing access, settlements, protected areas, and relevant land indicators where the data is available.

**[Show dossier or action list, if available.]**

> The expected output is a location dossier: what the team knows, what it infers, what needs confirmation, and who owns the next check. The benefit is that we find important questions earlier, reduce duplicated research, and give geology, legal, environment, and operations a shared starting point.
>
> Some monitoring, alerts, and automated reporting are partial or planned in the product notes. I will not describe those as live functions unless they are confirmed in the current deployment.
>
> The location is now worth technical investigation, subject to its checks. The next question is narrower: inside this area, where should we focus exploration effort?



[16:00-22:00]


**[Visual/demo: Open a prepared project. Show input inventory, aligned layers, map comparison, target ranking, evidence matrix, and a field-validation action. Clearly label fictional or sample data.]**

**Say:**

> Mariam's team has chosen a location to investigate. The exploration manager now asks: “Which geological targets deserve follow-up first?”
>
> TerraPulse brings supported satellite, geophysical, geochemical, and structural information into a shared geospatial workflow. The first step is not to press a ranking button. The first step is to check the inputs.

**[Show data inventory.]**

> We confirm the source, date, coordinate system, units, coverage, and quality of each input. If two surveys don't line up, we flag it. If coverage is missing, we record it. Data harmonization can save manual setup, but it cannot make weak inputs strong.

**[Toggle relevant layers.]**

> Now we inspect the layers. First separately, then together. Which patterns converge? Which conflict? Where do we have no evidence? We mark questions for a geologist to review.

**[Show the available ranking.]**

> The prospectivity or anomaly workflow helps prioritize candidates for attention. A higher rank is not a discovery probability. It is a reason to inspect the supporting evidence.

**[Open evidence matrix and discuss one support, one counter-signal, one gap.]**

> Here, we can see what supports this target, what weakens it, and what remains unknown. A qualified geologist now applies geological judgment. The team can advance it for field validation, collect more data, pause it, or reject it.

**[If target exports are supported, show a sample export and verify coordinate reference. Otherwise show a labeled concept.]**

> If the target advances, we prepare the field handoff: target summary, map, coordinates in a supported format, and the questions the field team should test. We verify coordinate reference and units before anyone relies on the file. Then field observations come back into the project for the next interpretation cycle.
>
> The expected result is not a bankable block model. It is an explainable, geologist-reviewed target shortlist and a practical validation plan. The benefit is less manual layer preparation and a better allocation of scarce sampling, survey, and drilling effort.
>
> TerraPulse supports exploration professionals. It does not replace field validation, established 3D modeling, or formal resource and reserve reporting.
>
> The target plan is ready to move into field discussion. Now the team needs to look at the site together, not as four different map versions.

>
> Which decision should change? What data supports it? Who reviews the output? Which handoff does it improve? And how will you measure the result?

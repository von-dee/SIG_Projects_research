# Stage Script: From Continent to Core

**Session:** The Intelligent Mine: Connecting AI Across the Full Value Chain, From First Drill Hole to Final Rehabilitation
**Format:** Technical session · solo, podium with deck · 30 minutes · no Q&A
**Based on:** `Investor_Flow_From_Continent_to_Core.md` (version 2)

---

## How to read this script

| Mark                      | Meaning                                                       |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **[SLIDE: …]**     | Change to this slide                                          |
| **[DEMO: …]**      | Do this in the product, live or from a recording              |
| **[pause]**         | Stop for a full two seconds. It will feel too long. It isn't. |
| *'Italic in quotes'*    | A character speaking. Slow down slightly.                     |
| *Italic without quotes* | A character's private thought. Drop your voice a little.      |

Each line is one breath. Short lines are meant to be short.

The spoken script is about 2,550 words, which is roughly 20 minutes at a steady pace (130 words a minute). The remaining eight to ten minutes are for demo actions, pauses and slide changes. If your demos run faster than expected, slow down in Sections 5 and 8, which carry the most weight. Rehearse the whole talk to 28:30.

## Section 1 · Opening (0:00–2:30)

> **Note:** This section takes control of the room and sets up the whole story. Don't start with "Good morning, thank you for having me." Start with the scene. Your name comes after the hook. The mandate line ("One country. One project. Eighteen months.") is the spine of the talk and returns in the close, so say it slowly. Introduce the four products by name only once, here, and promise honesty about the roadmap. That promise is what makes a technical audience trust everything after it.

**[SLIDE: Black. Then a boardroom at dawn, Johannesburg skyline through the window.]**

Johannesburg.
Monday, the second of March. Ten past eight in the morning.
A boardroom on the twentieth floor. Coffee nobody's drinking.
The chair of an investment committee leans across the table to his investment director.
*'Naledi. We've raised the fund. The mandate is gold, in Africa. One country. One project. You have eighteen months.'*
[pause]
Naledi nods.
And she thinks:
*Eighteen months. Last time, the desktop study alone took four. And it was out of date by the time we read it.*
[pause]

Good morning. I'm [your name], [one line of credentials].
Naledi is fictional. So is her fund.
But that sentence, *one country, one project, eighteen months*: some of you have heard it.
Some of you have said it.

**[SLIDE: Scattered tiles: spreadsheet, concession map, statute book, consultant report, hard drive, maintenance log. No lines between them.]**

Here's what Naledi is up against.
Not a lack of information. Too much of it, in too many places.
Production numbers in one spreadsheet. Concession maps in another office.
Licensing rules in a statute book. Environmental data with a consultant. Survey data on a hard drive.
And when the mine finally runs, every department has its own picture of it.
Where AI has arrived in mining, it's mostly been bolted onto one of these pieces at a time.
A smart tool in one office.
A smart island.

**[SLIDE: A funnel: Africa → Ghana → a ridge → a drill hole → a working mine → the land. Four product names beside it.]**

This session argues for something different.
One intelligence layer that carries a decision all the way down.
From a continent, to a country, to a ridge, to a drill hole, to a working mine.
And at the end, back to the land.
Four tools. MineSentinel. MineAsk. TerraPulse. And MyMine.
Each one hands its work to the next.
I'll show you each one doing real work.
And I'll tell you plainly what each one does today, and what's still on the roadmap.
[pause]
So, back to Naledi.
Eighteen months. The clock's running.

## Section 2 · MineSentinel: the continent (2:30–5:30)

The most important lines here are the neutral framing: "This isn't a ranking of nations." If you're presenting to a pan-African audience, never let a country sound like the loser. If MineSentinel's data for the other countries is thin, use the fallback version at the end of this script instead.

The first question is the biggest one.
Where in Africa?

## Section 3 · MineSentinel: zooming into Ghana (5:30–8:00)

> **Note:** This section moves quickly. It's a single, smooth zoom from country to region to three candidate areas. If time is tight, play it as a 30-second recording and talk over it. The key idea to land is in the last few lines: the boundaries move forward **as data**, not as a picture in an email. That's the first handoff in the chain, and the whole argument of the session depends on the audience noticing it.

**[DEMO: Zoom from Africa into Ghana.]**

So we zoom in.
Ghana's mines. Ghana's gold belts.

**[DEMO: Production by region.]**

Production by region.

**[DEMO: Zoom into the Western Region. Concessions on.]**

Into the Western Region. Concessions on.
Producing mines. And between them, gaps.
Open ground, with producing neighbours on either side.
Naledi marks three.

**[DEMO: GIS dashboard over the three areas.]**

On the GIS dashboard, she can see what's on each one.

She exports a one-page brief, and the three boundaries.

They go forward as data, into the next tool.
[pause]
Because a pin on a map is not an investment.

## Section 4 · MineAsk: three areas, three problems (8:00–10:30)

> **Note:** A new character, a new city, a new deadline. The phone call is a short PAST scene (place, action, speech, thought) and should feel like a scene change in a film. The "week becomes an afternoon" claim is the product team's estimate, so say "the product team estimates" every time. The three scorecards are the payoff: three areas, three different problems. Let the audience get ahead of you on "two are hard to fix, one you can design out". Give them a beat to work it out before you say it.

**[SLIDE: Accra, morning. A phone lighting up on a desk.]**

Accra.
Monday, the sixth of April. Nine in the morning.
Kwabena Asare, Kopano's man in Ghana, gets the call.
*'Kwabena, it's Naledi. Three areas in the Western Region. I need to know which one we can actually get, and what it takes. Title, licensing, environment, communities, roads. Three weeks.'*
*'Three weeks? For three areas?'*
*'Three weeks.'*
He hangs up.
And he thinks:
*That's a week per area. Just finding the cadastre records used to take me that long.*

**[DEMO: MineAsk. The three boundaries arrive from MineSentinel.]**

He opens MineAsk.
The three boundaries arrive from MineSentinel. He doesn't redraw anything.

**[DEMO: Type the question.]**

He types one question for each area:
*"Assess for a new gold project: title, licensing pathway, ESG exposure, communities, access."*

**[DEMO: Specialist agents run in parallel.]**

And specialist agents go out in parallel.
Cadastre. Extractive-industry transparency records. Satellite. Environment. Logistics. Valuation.
The product team estimates that a week of record-pulling becomes an afternoon.
Treat that as a hypothesis we measure in pilots.
But watch the work change shape.

**[DEMO: Three scorecards, side by side.]**

Three scorecards, side by side. Same criteria for each.
Krofa Valley: good access. But the cadastre shows an overlapping claim with a neighbour. Disputed ground.
Asempa Flats: clean title. But satellite mapping shows heavy illegal small-scale mining on the ground already.
Adwenkrom Ridge: clean title. Strong geology. Producing neighbours.
And one red flag.
[pause]
Two of those problems are hard to fix.
One of them, you can design out.
So Kwabena goes deep on Adwenkrom.

## Section 5 · MineAsk: the hard stop, and the regulator's desk (10:30–15:00)

> **Note:** This is the longest section and carries half of the session's argument: "stays aligned with the regulator." It has two halves. The first is the investor finding and fixing the problem (the red flag and the redraw). The second is the scene at the Minerals Commission, where the regulator sees the same evidence. Milk Gifty's thought; it's the emotional centre of the section. The line "I'm not going to tell you this makes approvals faster" is deliberate: in a room full of regulators and operators, refusing to overclaim builds more trust than any feature. Check the Act 703 stages with counsel before the event.

**[DEMO: Open the red flag in the map studio.]**

Here's the red flag.
The eastern block of Adwenkrom crosses a forest-reserve buffer.
And a stream that's already under water stress.
In the old world, you find this out when the inspector arrives.
Or when the community does.
Usually after the money has moved.
Here, we find it before anyone has moved a single vehicle.

**[DEMO: Redraw the boundary to exclude the eastern block.]**

So Kwabena redraws the boundary.
The eastern block comes out.
[pause]
Some gold will stay in the ground because of that line.
And that's fine.
The forest was there first.

**[DEMO: Open the licensing pathway.]**

Now, the licensing pathway.
This is Ghana's Minerals and Mining Act, Act 703, laid out as steps.
From prospecting through to a mining lease. What's needed at each stage. Where the hard stops are.
And these are rules a lawyer can read and check.
It's not legal advice.
It's a map that tells your lawyer exactly where to look.

**[DEMO: Quick pass through title, ESG, community and logistics.]**

Title status and expiry, from the cadastre.
Water, forest and protected areas.
The communities around the ridge, and their history.
And the road out: two haul corridors to port, each scored for risk.
One honest line here.
Continuous monitoring and automated alerts are on the roadmap. Today, this runs on demand.

**[DEMO: Source and confidence on a finding. Then the "must verify" list. Then export.]**

And then the part that matters most for trust.
Every finding shows where it came from, and how confident it is.
And there's a list of what must still be verified, at the official registry and in the field.
Kwabena exports a memo.
Naledi reads it on the flight home, and texts the chair:
*'Adwenkrom Ridge. Eastern block excluded. Licence pathway mapped. Recommend we proceed.'*
The reply comes before she lands.
*'Approved.'*
[pause]

**[SLIDE: An office at the Minerals Commission, Accra. A file open on a desk.]**

Now. This session promised that the intelligence layer stays aligned with the regulator.
So let's go to the other side of the desk.
Minerals Commission, Accra.
Tuesday, the twelfth of May. Eleven in the morning.
Gifty Amoah, a licensing officer, opens the Kopano application.
And it's not just a form. It's the evidence pack.
The boundary. The eastern block, left out. The forest buffer, drawn in green.
Every source, with a date.
She checks it against her own district view.
The buffer matches.
[pause]
And she thinks:
*Most applications tell me what they want. This one also shows me what it left out. And why.*
[pause]
She writes in the file:
*Boundary consistent with forest reserve buffer. Proceed to next stage.*
[pause]
I'm not going to tell you this makes approvals faster.
I can't promise that. Nobody should.
What it does is put the investor and the regulator in front of the same evidence.
That's what "aligned with the regulator" looks like in practice.

## Section 6 · TerraPulse: thirty anomalies, eight holes (15:00–20:00)

> **Note:** The field camp is the most intimate scene in the talk: night, a generator, a satellite phone. Let the room feel the pressure in Efua's thought ("there won't be a second programme"). The section's peak is the evidence matrix and "She clicks reject". Pause before and after it. The $62,500 figure must be called illustrative out loud and on the slide. The committee scene at the end is short but important: Naledi answers the chair from the evidence, not from memory. That's the trust payoff of explainable AI.

**[SLIDE: A field camp at night. One bulb. A satellite phone on a camp table.]**

The licence comes through.
Three months later. A field camp below the ridge.
Sunday, the ninth of August. Twenty to ten at night. The generator's humming.
Dr Efua Mensah, the exploration manager, is going through survey data when the satellite phone rings.
It's Naledi.
*'Efua, the committee meets on the twentieth. They've approved eight holes. Which eight?'*
Efua looks at her screen.
Thirty anomalies.
And she thinks:
*Thirty anomalies. Eight holes. If the first two come up dry, there won't be a second programme.*

**[DEMO: TerraPulse. Open the project; the boundary arrives from MineAsk.]**

She opens TerraPulse.
The project boundary is the one Kwabena redrew. The forest buffer is already out.
Nobody redrew it.

**[DEMO: Upload four datasets. Harmonise.]**

Her data arrives the way it always does.
Aeromagnetics from one contractor. Soil geochemistry from a lab. Satellite alteration. An old structural map.
Four formats. Different coordinate systems.
Normally, that's weeks of cleanup before anyone does any geology.
TerraPulse harmonises it into one map.

**[DEMO: Turn the layers on one at a time.]**

Layer by layer, the evidence lines up.

**[DEMO: Choose a gold deposit model. Run the analysis. Ranked targets.]**

She picks a gold deposit model and runs the analysis.
Thirty anomalies become a ranked list.
But a ranking without reasons is a black box.
And no geologist puts a rig on a black box.

**[DEMO: Open the evidence matrix on the biggest anomaly.]**

So every target shows its working.
Here's the biggest anomaly on the ridge. The one everyone would have drilled first.
Supporting: very strong magnetics.
Conflicting: the soil geochemistry doesn't agree.
Missing: no IP survey.
[pause]
Efua looks at it for a long time.
[pause]
She clicks reject.
[pause]
And she types the reason. So the next geologist knows why.
The most important button on this screen isn't "run analysis".
It's "reject".
The machine suggests. The geologist decides.

**[DEMO: Generate the board report. Export coordinates to the field GPS.]**

She builds a board report from the same evidence.
And sends the coordinates straight to the field crew's GPS.

**[SLIDE: One drill hole: 250 m × $250/m = $62,500. Label: ILLUSTRATIVE.]**

Here's why that matters.
Illustratively, and this is the product team's own example:
one 250-metre hole at 250 dollars a metre is 62,500 dollars.
Avoid one weak hole, and you've paid for the system many times over.
And now the honest part.
TerraPulse doesn't guarantee discoveries.
It doesn't produce a JORC or NI 43-101 resource. Your competent person does that, with the data it hands over.

**[SLIDE: The committee room again.]**

Thursday, the twentieth of August.
The committee reads Efua's report.
The chair asks one question.
*'Why not the big anomaly?'*
Naledi doesn't answer from memory.
She turns to the evidence, and reads Efua's note.
The chair nods.
*'Approved.'*
[pause]
The holes hit. The resource is estimated.
A mine is built.

## Section 7 · MyMine: the investor on the bench (20:00–25:00)

> **Note:** This is the section most at risk on the day. MyMine's features are stated by the product owner but haven't been confirmed in a demo, and remote assist is unverified. Run anything unconfirmed from a recording, and say "the use cases we're piloting." The network-drop moment is a gift if it works live, so rehearse it. The section ends on the strongest callback in the talk: Naledi asking whether the bench is inside the forest line, with the MineAsk boundary still there. Slow right down for "Africa. To Ghana. To a ridge…"

**[SLIDE: Johannesburg, early morning. A laptop and a coffee.]**

Two years later.
Johannesburg. Monday, seven in the morning.
Naledi's at her desk with a coffee.
Four thousand kilometres away, Adwenkrom is in its first months of production.
She opens a browser. And she joins the Monday planning review.
She thinks:
*Two years ago, this was a pin on a map. Now I'm about to stand on the bench.*

**[DEMO: Open the MyMine 3D twin of the pit.]**

This is MyMine.
The mine, in 3D. Built from its own survey, CAD and GIS files.

The geotech's in a headset.

Same model. Same moment.

Yaw highlights the bench they'll mine this week.
Everyone sees it.

**[DEMO: Switch on one or two live feeds.]**

One or two live feeds from the site.

Now, anyone who's run a remote site knows the link drops.

The session keeps running.

**[DEMO or RECORDING: HSE walk-through of hazard zones.]**

And Abena, the safety manager, walks new crews through the hazard zones in 3D, before they ever stand in one.
Plan-versus-actual overlays and deeper fleet integration are on the roadmap.
We're not replacing your planning suite.
We're giving it a room.

**[DEMO: Switch on the inherited layers one by one.]**

Now watch what's inside the model.
The district picture, from MineSentinel.
The boundary and the forest buffer, from MineAsk.
Efua's targets, and her reasons, from TerraPulse.
Naledi unmutes.
*'Yaw. That bench. Is it inside the line we drew around the forest?'*
Yaw switches on the buffer.
It appears, untouched, two hundred metres east.
*'Yes. Well inside.'*
[pause]
And Naledi thinks:
*Africa. To Ghana. To a ridge. To a hole. To this bench.*
*And every step is still here.*
[pause]
But every mine ends.

## Section 8 · Closure: "Did they?" (25:00–27:30)

> **Note:** This is the emotional peak of the talk and the reason this version exists: the session title ends at "Final Rehabilitation". Slow down. Drop your volume. The old man's question ("Did they?") gets a full pause on both sides. Keep the claims exact: the layers show vegetation and forest change and water stress, not water quality, so never let him ask whether the river is safe to drink. Say the roadmap line about 4D history plainly, then go straight to the final image: the line that protected the forest is now the proof the promise was kept.

**[SLIDE: A roadside community office in a village. Morning light.]**

Fourteen years after the first licence.
A Saturday morning.
A village at the foot of the ridge.
An old man, Opanin Kwaku Badu, walks to the mine's community office by the road.
The liaison officer, Ama, is unlocking the door.
*'Ama. My grandfather farmed on that ridge. When the mine came, they told us the trees would come back.'*
[pause]
*'Did they?'*
[pause]
In the old world, Ama would say: *"Let me find the report, Opanin."*
And the report would be a year old, and written by hand.
Instead, she opens the same map where Naledi first spotted this ridge.

**[DEMO or SLIDE: The ridge on MineSentinel's map. Forest-change and water layers. The forest buffer.]**

The forest-change layer, over the ground they mined and backfilled.
The water context for the stream.
And the forest buffer. The line Kwabena drew on day one.
She turns the screen toward him.
The ridge, fourteen years apart.
Green, coming back across the backfilled ground.
And the eastern forest, never touched.
He looks at it for a long time.
[pause]
*'The trees came back.'*
[pause]
Now, honestly: some of this, like the twin's full history and audit trail, is still on the roadmap.
But look at the shape of it.
The line drawn to protect the forest at the very start
is the evidence, at the very end, that the promise was kept.

## Section 9 · Close and the ask (27:30–30:00)

> **Note:** There's no Q&A, so this section has to do three jobs: sum up the journey in one breath, give the audience something practical to take home (the five questions), and make one clear, low-risk ask (one site, one question, one baseline). The final line brings back both deadlines: Naledi's eighteen months and the elder's fourteen years. After "Thank you", stop. Don't add a summary or a joke. Walk off, or hold still until the applause starts.

**[SLIDE: The funnel again, now fully lit: Africa → Ghana → ridge → hole → mine → land.]**

One country. One project. Eighteen months.
We went from a continent, to a country, to a ridge, to a drill hole, to a working mine.
And back to the land.
Four tools.
And not one of them had to start from scratch.
Each one started from what the last one knew.
That's the difference between AI in a department,
and an intelligent mine.

**[SLIDE: Five questions, appearing one at a time.]**

So here's what I'd ask you to take home.
Before you buy any AI system for your operation, ask it five questions.
Does it start from what the last stage already knows?
Does it hand its work on, so nobody retypes it?
Does it show its evidence: the source, the date, how confident it is?
Does it keep the regulator in the loop?
And does it work where your mine actually is: in the dust, offline, on a tablet?

**[SLIDE: QR code and contact details.]**

If you'd like to test one stage of this with us, one site, one question, measured against your own baseline, the code is on the screen.
[pause]
Naledi had eighteen months.
Opanin Kwaku Badu waited fourteen years for his answer.
[pause]
The tools you choose today decide how fast, and how honestly, you can answer them both.
[pause]
Thank you.

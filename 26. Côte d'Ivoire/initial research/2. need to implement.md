
Based on how Ghana built it, Côte d'Ivoire would need these things. I'm confident of the Ghana side; for Ivorian specifics I've marked what to confirm in the meeting.

## 1. Legal and policy foundation

- **A binding rule on equipment.** This would be a decree, regulation or ministerial order requiring registration of earth-moving equipment used in mining, restricting it to licensed areas, and setting penalties such as seizure. Ghana's LI 2404 does this.
- **Legal power for the regulator** to fit trackers, access equipment, and act on violations. Confirm whether the current mining code (and its small-scale or semi-mechanised permit categories) already gives the directorate this authority or needs a new instrument.
- **Rules on data and privacy** for GPS tracking of private owners' machines, and on evidence admissibility for alerts.

## 2. Institutional set-up

- **A lead agency and a mandate**, probably the mining directorate, with a named project owner.
- **Inter-agency agreements** with customs, the transport and vehicle registration authority, local government, the ports authority and security forces. Ghana's system works because these agencies act on what the control room finds.
- **A response protocol:** who is called, within what time, and with what powers when an alert fires.

## 3. Data

- **A digitised, GPS-referenced register of all valid licences and concessions.** This is the base layer for geo-fencing.
- **An inventory of existing equipment** (owners, types, chassis and registration numbers) and a way to capture new imports at the border and port.
- **Clear definitions of permitted areas**, including buffers, forest reserves and no-go zones.

## 4. Technology

- **Tamper-resistant GPS trackers** suited to local conditions (vibration, dust, heat, battery life).
- **A tracking and geo-fencing platform** that pairs each machine to its licence and generates alerts.
- **A control room** with 24/7 staffing, hardware, power backup, connectivity, and a hotline or call centre.
- **Data hosting and security**, ideally in-country, with backups and user access controls.
- **Connectivity** (mobile or satellite) in remote mining zones, which needs checking before pilot sites are chosen.
- **Drones and AI imagery**, if included, add their own needs: pilots, flight permissions, image processing and a way to link findings to enforcement.

## 5. People and capacity

- Control room operators and supervisors.
- Field teams to install and inspect trackers, with security escort in risky zones.
- Trainers and a French-language curriculum, plus a handover plan so the directorate can run the system without the vendor.

## 6. Money

- **Upfront costs:** devices, installation, platform set-up, control room build-out.
- **Running costs:** airtime or data, device replacement, staff, maintenance, licensing.
- **A funding model:** state budget, donor or climate-finance support, or a fee charged to equipment owners. Ghana's minister explicitly called for a sustainability plan, so decide this early.

## 7. Delivery plan

- Needs assessment, then a pilot in selected regions, then phased national rollout, with agreed success measures at each stage (machines registered, share of alerts acted on, violations reduced).
- Communication to miners and equipment dealers before enforcement starts, since compliance depends on people understanding the rules.

## What to confirm with them first

1. Whether the current legal framework is enough or a new regulation is required, and how long that would take.
2. Whether licence data is digitised and accurate.
3. Which agencies they can get to commit.
4. Who pays.

If the legal basis and funding are unresolved, you can still do the needs assessment, but the pilot will be hard to launch.

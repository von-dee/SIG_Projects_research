# Côte d'Ivoire: Mining and Minerals Note (as of 21 October 2026)

## 1. Snapshot

- **Gold dominates.** Output is expected to reach 62 t in 2026, up from 59.33 t in 2025, with 14 gold mines operating. Output was 23.54 t in 2015, and the government expects 63 t in 2026 and 65 t in 2027. Note the sources differ on the count of operating mines (13, 14, or "nine" per Montage's release), likely because of definitions and the inclusion of satellite pits.
- **Target:** the national goal is 100 t of gold a year over the coming decade.
- **Manganese is the second pillar.** Côte d'Ivoire ranked eighth in world manganese output in 2024, with 1.8% of global production.
- **Mining's economic weight:** mining, including oil and gas, was 3.4% of GDP in 2022. The government wants mining (excluding oil and gas) to rise from about 3% of GDP in 2018 to 6% by 2025. I did not find confirmation that this was reached.

## 2. Geology and Mineral Deposits

**Geological base.** The country holds about 35% of West Africa's greenstone belts (the Birimian system that hosts gold in Ghana, Burkina Faso, Mali and Guinea).

**Gold.** National gold potential is estimated at about 600 t, spread across the north, centre, west and south-east. Belts and regions to know:

- **North:** Tongon, Sissingué/Fimbiasso, Koné (Montage), Lafigué (Dabakala), Séguéla (Worodougou)
- **Centre:** Yaouré, Bonikro/Hiré, Agbaou, Didievi
- **West:** Ity
- **East/south-east:** Tanda-Iguela (Assafou), near the Ghana border; Afema

**Manganese.** Potential is estimated at 12 Mt, with four mines operating at end-2024 at Bondoukou, Guitry, Kaniasso and Lagnonkaha. Production peaked at about 1.33 Mt in 2020 and fell 12% in 2024 on declining ore grades at the Shiloh and Bondoukou mines. The ministry projects 1.14 Mt in 2025 and 1.25 Mt in 2026. The recovery depends on IMMSA restarting in Odienné.

**Nickel (and cobalt/copper).** Lateritic nickel potential is estimated at 260 Mt. Production is dominated by the Foungouesso and Moyango open-pit mines in the Biankouma-Touba area (Compagnie Minière du Bafing), plus the Sipilou North licence held by a sister company. Samapleu is a nickel-copper project run by Sama Resources with SODEMI. A 2026 report says nickel is now the second most exploited metal, with the Sipilou nickel-cobalt mine (NOCI/IC Nickel) due to enter production from 2026. Treat that timing as unconfirmed.

**Bauxite.** Lagune Exploitation Bongouanou produces bauxite exported to China. The Bénéné deposit is estimated at 35 Mt of ore, in the Moronou region.

**Iron ore.** Resources of about 4,000 Mt are cited in the west. This is undeveloped, and the infrastructure and capital needs are large.

**Diamonds.** Reserves are estimated at 11 million carats, but production is entirely artisanal. The diamond ban was lifted in 2014 and the country is Kimberley Process compliant.

**Critical minerals (early stage).** Exploration has identified copper, lithium, coltan, cobalt and some rare earths. Permits for lithium, nickel, cobalt and rare earths were granted from 2024, and further lithium and rare earths permits were granted in January 2025. Nothing here is near production.

## 4. Regional Overview

- **Rank:** Côte d'Ivoire was Africa's seventh-largest gold producer in 2024 (World Gold Council). GlobalData expects it to become West Africa's third-largest gold producer in 2026, surpassing Mali. That rests on Mali's 2025 disruption and its forecast 28% rebound, so the overtaking is fragile.
- **Scale versus Ghana:** Ghana, Africa's largest producer, had output of 187 t in 2025. Ghana's formal artisanal purchases alone were up to 54 t in H1 2026, which shows how much Ghana's small-scale sector dwarfs Ivorian figures. The gap is mostly ASM formalisation, not industrial output.
- **Risk comparison:** Côte d'Ivoire is seen as comparatively politically stable relative to several Sahel neighbours, while illegal mining, land disputes and community expectations remain issues. Mali's convention dispute and Burkina Faso's tightening of terms have pushed investors toward Côte d'Ivoire.
- **Resource nationalism trend:** Guinea banned raw gold exports in June 2026, and Mali and Niger are building refineries. Côte d'Ivoire is on the same direction of travel, but with no export ban to date.
- **Cross-border issues:** Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana run a joint monitoring arrangement on pollution of the Bia and Tanoé rivers from illegal mining. Ghana's formalisation drive also aims to redirect gold that leaks out via Mali or Burkina Faso to the UAE.

## 5. Regulatory Overview

**Legal framework**

- **Mining Code:** Law No. 2014-138 of 24 March 2014, with implementing decree 2014-97 of 25 June 2014.
- **Regional layer:** As a UEMOA member, Côte d'Ivoire is also subject to the Community Mining Code (Regulation 02/2023/CM/UEMOA).
- **Related laws:** the Environment Code (1996), Water Code (1998), Rural Land Code (1998), Forestry Code (2014), customs code, 2018 Investment Code, and the Kimberley Process certificate rules.

**Institutions.** The Council of Ministers decides on granting and withdrawing mining titles. Recent examples are the four exploration permits adopted on 22 July 2026 and three more on 5 August 2026. Since September 2025 an e-cadastre portal has digitalised title management. Permit grants have risen: 151 of 189 applications in 2023, 160 of 203 in 2024, and 171 of 225 in 2025.

**Title regime**

- Exploration permit applicants must be incorporated in Côte d'Ivoire.
- The State can require a local partner in the capital of an exploitation permit holder. In practice, the State takes a 10% non-contributing (free-carried) interest, and SODEMI often holds a further 5–10%, as at Ity and Lafigué.
- Exploration permits run for four years.
- A mining convention is negotiated for each major project. Endeavour says its negotiations proceed under the 2014 Code with a 10% free carry expected.

**Fiscal terms**

- **Royalties:** the gold royalty is a sliding rate of 3% to 6% of sales, plus a profit share tied to the government's stake.
- **2025 increase:** the 2025 finance law raised the ad valorem tax on gold by two percentage points, and its tax annex disapplies the Code's fiscal stability article (Art. 164) for those revisions. This is the most important change for investors: stability guarantees did not protect against it. Perseus reports having paid $20M of additional royalties, including amounts for earlier periods.
- **Tax holiday:** the 2014 Code gave a five-year corporate income-tax exemption from first commercial production. Perseus's has now lapsed, so expect a higher effective tax burden across the first-wave mines.

**Code revision.** The minister announced in July 2024 that the code would be revised to increase state profits. Submission of a draft to the Government Secretariat General was planned for November 2025. I did not find evidence that a new code has been enacted: Endeavour was still describing negotiations under the 2014 Code, and local content provisions are to be folded into the revised code. The practical read: expect a revised code with a larger state share, local content rules, and possibly local processing requirements, but verify current status with counsel.

**Policy direction**

- **PIRME 2026-2040:** an integrated mineral and energy policy built around local processing and industrial upgrading.
- **Local content:** a GIZ-funded 2022 assessment found the national employment target had been exceeded, with 94% of employees Ivorian by 2019, but gaps remain in supply and subcontracting.
- **Gold refining and ASM:** SIMEP will operate semi-industrial mines, a national gold buying office, and a refinery of about 100 t a year, with the state holding 10% and SODEMI 39%. The refinery in Abidjan is due to start after H1 2027. The partnership with StoneX was approved at a presidential council in June 2025.
- **ASM:** a national artisanal mining rationalisation programme has set up 100 local technical committees (2014-2024). Many artisanal operators still work informally.

## 6. Key Takeaways

1. **Growth runway:** new volumes come from Koné (2026-27), Séguéla and Lafigué expansions, and Assafou (2028). That makes 65 t plausible, but 100 t by 2030 looks like a stretch without more big projects.
2. **Fiscal squeeze:** higher royalties, expiring tax holidays and a pending code revision point to a rising state take.
3. **Domestication:** Tongon's sale to an Ivorian group, SODEMI stakes, SIMEP and local content rules all signal a push for local ownership and value capture.
4. **Diversification is slow:** manganese is the only established non-gold line, nickel is emerging, and lithium, iron ore and rare earths are early stage.
5. **Risks:** the legal stability promised by the 2014 Code has proven partial, artisanal mining is a persistent problem, and manganese output is exposed to grade and logistics issues.

**Caveats:** Several figures come from ministry projections and trade press rather than audited data, and production counts and mine counts differ between sources. I can turn this into a formatted Word document or a slide deck if you'd like.

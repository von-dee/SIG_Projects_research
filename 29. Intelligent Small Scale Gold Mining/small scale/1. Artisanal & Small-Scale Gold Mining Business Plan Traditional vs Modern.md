# Artisanal & Small-Scale Gold Mining Business Plan: Traditional vs Modern

Oct 28, 2026 · @BrandWatch

## Executive summary

A modern, mercury-free small-scale gold operation recovers roughly twice the gold of a traditional one from the same ore, and earns about 2.3 times the operating profit, for about 14 times the upfront capital. The recommended path is a staged upgrade: start with mercury-free gravity concentration and borax smelting, then add leaching and full water and tailings controls once cash flow and grade are proven.

The plan compares three operating models on the same reference deposit: 10 tonnes of ore per day at 5 g/t gold, 300 operating days a year (3,000 t of ore, 15,000 g of contained gold).

| Model | Upfront capital (USD) | Gold recovery | Gold sold per year | Operating profit per year (USD) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional (manual, mercury) | \~45,000 | 30–40% | \~6,000 g | \~424,000 |
| Stage 1: mercury-free gravity | \~160,000 | 55–65% | \~9,000 g | \~713,000 |
| Stage 2: modern full plant | \~650,000 | 80–90% | \~12,750 g | \~977,000 |

All figures are illustrative planning estimates, in US dollars, at an assumed gold price of USD 3,500/oz (about USD 112.5/g). Replace them with the current spot price, your measured ore grade and local labour, fuel and royalty rates before using this plan with lenders or regulators.

The traditional model looks cheap but carries hidden costs: 50–70% of the gold is lost in tailings, gold is sold at a heavy discount to informal buyers, mercury exposure harms workers and communities, and the operation risks closure or confiscation. The modern model turns the same ore body into a bankable, licensable business that can sell to refineries and certified ethical-gold buyers at near-spot prices.

## Business concept, market and legal context

The business mines and processes gold from an alluvial or hard-rock deposit and sells doré bars to a licensed refinery or authorised buyer. Gold has a deep global market, so the business risk lies in production, cost and compliance, not in finding customers.

**Business model**

- Legal form: a registered company or a formalised cooperative of miners, holding a small-scale or artisanal mining licence for a defined concession.
- Revenue: sale of doré (typically 70–90% gold) priced off the London spot price, less refining charges and buyer margin.
- Secondary revenue: toll-processing ore from neighbouring licensed miners, selling aggregate from waste rock, and certified ethical-gold premiums.

**Market and buyers**

| Buyer channel | Typical price received | Requirements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Informal local trader | 75–90% of spot | None, but cash-only and legal risk |
| Licensed national buyer or central bank programme | 90–97% of spot | Licence, production records |
| International refinery | 95–99% of spot | Licence, traceability, OECD due-diligence checks |
| Certified ethical gold (Fairmined, Fairtrade) | Spot plus a premium (around USD 2,000–4,000/kg, approximate) | Mercury-free or controlled processes, audits, labour standards |

**Legal and licensing checklist**

1. Mining licence or permit for the concession, plus landowner and community agreements.
2. Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) or Environmental Management Plan, with a reclamation bond where required.
3. Water-use permit and discharge limits.
4. Explosives permits: blasting only by a licensed blaster with licensed storage, or contracted out to a licensed firm.
5. Chemical permits for cyanide purchase, storage and use, aligned with the International Cyanide Management Code.
6. Mercury: the Minamata Convention bans whole-ore amalgamation, open burning of amalgam and cyanide leaching of mercury-contaminated tailings, and requires national plans to reduce and eliminate mercury in mining. Many countries now ban it outright; this plan assumes zero mercury in the modern model.
7. Tax and royalty registration, export permits and gold-trading licences.
8. Occupational health and safety registration, worker contracts and no child labour.

These rules differ by country. Confirm each item with the national mining ministry or regulator before committing capital.

## Mining methods: traditional vs modern

Modern methods win on exploration and planning before they win on equipment: knowing where the gold is cuts more cost than any machine.

**Exploration and planning (both deposit types)**

| Stage | Traditional | Modern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Finding gold | Word of mouth, test pits, panning samples | Drone photogrammetry and LiDAR maps, handheld magnetometers, soil geochemistry |
| Measuring grade | Visual estimate, trial panning | Portable XRF, fire assay at a lab, systematic pit or auger sampling on a grid |
| Mine plan | Dig where gold was found last | GIS map of grade blocks; mine the richest blocks first, plan waste dumps and backfill |
| Records | None or a notebook | Daily production log on a phone or tablet, tied to assay results |

### A. Alluvial (placer) mining

Gold sits in loose river gravels, terraces or surface soils, so the work is moving and washing material.

| Element | Traditional | Modern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Excavation | Shovels, hoes, head-pans; deep unprotected pits | Small excavator (5–20 t) or backhoe; benched, sloped pit walls |
| Washing | Hand panning, hand-fed wooden sluices | Hydraulic sluicing with water monitors, trommel screen to remove oversize, gravel pump |
| Gold capture | Sacks or blankets on sluices; mercury added to pans | Riffled sluice boxes with modern gold-trapping mats (e.g. vortex or nugget-trap matting), then a centrifugal concentrator on fines |
| River work | Divers with suction hoses | Small licensed suction dredges with settling barges, only where permitted |
| Throughput | 1–5 m³ of gravel per day per team | 50–300 m³ per day per wash plant |
| Land after mining | Open pits filled with stagnant water | Topsoil stockpiled, pits backfilled and replanted as mining moves along |

### B. Hard-rock (primary) mining

Gold is locked in quartz veins or host rock, so the work is breaking, hoisting and crushing rock.

| Element | Traditional | Modern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Access | Narrow hand-dug shafts, often unsupported | Engineered shaft or decline with timber or steel supports and rock bolts |
| Rock breaking | Hammers, chisels, fire-setting, unlicensed explosives | Pneumatic jackhammers or battery rock drills; controlled blasting only by a licensed blaster |
| Hoisting | Hand windlass, buckets on ropes | Electric or diesel haulage winch with safety brake; ore cars on rails for drives |
| Ventilation | None; workers exposed to dust and fumes | Mechanical fans and ducting; wet drilling to keep down silica dust |
| Selectivity | Large amounts of waste mixed with ore | Follow the vein using grade-control sampling to send less waste to the mill |
| Throughput | 0.5–3 t of ore per day | 10–50 t per day |

**Choosing a method.** Alluvial deposits need less capital and pay back faster but run out quicker and disturb more land per gram. Hard-rock deposits last longer and suit a fixed processing plant, but need more capital, more safety controls and a measured resource before investment.

## Processing and recovery chain: traditional vs modern

Processing is where most money is won or lost: a traditional circuit leaves 50–70% of the gold in its tailings, while a well-run modern circuit loses 10–20%.

The chain is the same in both models; the tools and the recovery at each step differ.

1. **Size reduction (comminution).** Liberates gold grains from rock.
   - Traditional: hand crushing with hammers, then hammer mills or stamp mills run dry, producing uneven particle size and heavy silica dust.
   - Modern: jaw crusher to about 10 mm, then a ball mill run wet in closed circuit with a classifier or hydrocyclone, grinding to a target size (often 75–150 microns) set by test work. Wet milling also controls dust.
2. **Gravity concentration.** Uses gold's density (about 19 g/cm³ versus about 2.7 for quartz).
   - Traditional: sluices lined with sacks or blankets, then panning; fine gold washes away.
   - Modern: centrifugal concentrator (Knelson, Falcon or iCon type) on the mill discharge, a shaking table to upgrade the concentrate, and jigs for coarser material. Concentrate is a small, high-grade mass that is easy to secure.
3. **Extraction and refining.**
   - Traditional: mercury amalgamation of whole ore or concentrate, then burning the amalgam in the open. Recovers only coarse gold, releases mercury vapour and leaves mercury-contaminated tailings.
   - Modern, high-grade concentrate: direct smelting with borax flux. No toxic chemicals, low cost, and recovery on clean concentrates is comparable to or better than amalgamation.
   - Modern, fine gold in tailings: cyanide vat leaching or carbon-in-leach (CIL) tanks with activated carbon, operated under a licence, in lined vats, with cyanide destruction before tailings disposal. Lifts total recovery to 80–90%.
   - Emerging lower-toxicity options: glycine-based and thiosulfate leaching, and halide (chlorine-based) processes. They are promising but need ore-specific test work and are less proven at small scale.
4. **Smelting and off-take.**
   - Traditional: crude melting with charcoal or a blow torch; low-purity sponge gold sold for cash.
   - Modern: induction or gas-fired crucible furnace with borax, soda ash and silica flux, producing weighed, sampled and numbered doré bars sold with assay certificates to a licensed buyer or refinery.

**Recovery by step (reference ore, 5 g/t)**

| Circuit | Gravity recovery | Leach recovery from tailings | Total recovery | Gold recovered per tonne |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional (hammer mill, sluice, mercury) | 30–40% | None | 30–40% | 1.5–2.0 g |
| Stage 1: ball mill, centrifugal concentrator, shaking table, borax smelting | 55–65% | None | 55–65% | 2.75–3.25 g |
| Stage 2: Stage 1 plus CIL or vat leach on tailings | 55–65% | 25–30% of feed | 80–90% | 4.0–4.5 g |

Recovery depends strongly on the ore. Before buying equipment, send a 50–100 kg bulk sample for gravity-recoverable-gold and leach tests at a metallurgical lab; this costs a few thousand dollars and avoids buying the wrong plant.

## Operational and environmental infrastructure

Infrastructure is what keeps a modern site licensed and running: water, tailings, power and reclamation together take about 40% of Stage 2 capital and pay for themselves through recycled water, lower fuel bills and avoided fines.

| Area | Traditional | Modern | Cost-saving angle |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Water | Drawn straight from the river; muddy water returned untreated | Storage basin, 3–4 stage settling ponds, closed-loop recirculation (70–90% of water reused) | Less pumping and fewer permit problems in the dry season |
| Tailings | Dumped on land or in streams, often with mercury | Lined tailings pond or engineered TSF; cyanide destroyed before discharge; dry-stacking where water is scarce | Tailings stay available for re-processing as recovery improves |
| Power | Small diesel engines on each machine | Solar–diesel hybrid microgrid with batteries, or grid connection | Diesel is often 25–40% of operating cost; solar cuts it by half or more |
| Reclamation | None; pits abandoned | Concurrent reclamation: topsoil stockpiled, pits backfilled, land replanted as mining advances | Spreads cost over the mine life and protects the licence and reclamation bond |
| Security | Gold handled by many people | Locked concentrate room, CCTV, sealed concentrate cans, two-person rule | Cuts theft, often a hidden 5–15% loss |
| Site layout | Unplanned | Mine, plant, ponds, camp and stores laid out to shorten haul and pumping distances | Lower fuel and labour per tonne |

**Modern-innovation highlights**

- Modular, skid-mounted or containerised plants that can be moved to a new deposit.
- Solar hybrid power with remote monitoring of fuel use and generator hours.
- Water-level and turbidity sensors on settling ponds.
- Phytoremediation (planting vetiver and other grasses) to stabilise pit walls and old tailings.
- Digital traceability of each bar, linking mine, date, weight and assay, which buyers increasingly require.

Traditional mining wins only on entry cost and simplicity; modern mining wins on every measure that decides long-term profit.

## How to minimise cost

The largest savings come from not spending on the wrong ore or the wrong plant, then from energy, mining selectivity and maintenance.

| Strategy | How | Typical saving |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Test before you buy | Grid sampling, assays and a metallurgical bulk test before ordering equipment | Avoids six-figure mistakes in plant size or process |
| Stage the investment | Start with Stage 1 gravity and borax; add leaching only when grade and cash flow justify it | Cuts upfront capital by about 75% |
| Buy used or refurbished | Reconditioned crushers, mills and generators from closing mines or dealers | 30–50% off new prices |
| Lease, don't buy, mobile fleet | Rent excavators and compressors by the hour or month | Converts capital to variable cost; no idle-machine cost |
| Switch to solar hybrid power | Solar plus batteries for daytime milling, diesel as backup | 40–60% lower fuel spend; fuel is often a quarter or more of costs |
| Mine selectively | Follow veins with grade control; screen out barren gravel before washing | Less waste hauled, crushed and ground per gram |
| Recycle water | Closed-loop recirculation from settling ponds | Lower pumping cost and no dry-season shutdowns |
| Preventive maintenance | Daily checklists, scheduled liner and bearing changes, critical spares on site | Fewer breakdowns; downtime is the costliest hidden expense |
| Bulk and group purchasing | Cooperatives buy fuel, grinding balls, borax and PPE together | 10–20% on consumables |
| Train a smaller, skilled crew | Fewer, better-paid operators with clear roles and bonuses | Higher output per worker, lower accident and theft losses |
| Shared or toll processing | Use a central licensed plant instead of building one, or sell spare capacity | Avoids capital or earns extra revenue |
| Stay compliant | Permits, safety and environmental controls in place from day one | Avoids fines, closure and confiscation, the most expensive outcome |

## How to maximise profit

Every 10 percentage points of extra recovery on the reference deposit adds about 1,500 g of gold, worth roughly USD 160,000 a year, at almost no extra cost, so recovery is the first profit lever.

1. **Raise recovery.** Grind to the size test work specifies, run centrifugal concentrators on mill discharge, clean concentrates on a shaking table, and leach fine gold in tailings. Assay tailings weekly; if they carry more than about 0.5 g/t, fix the circuit.
2. **Get a better price.** Sell to licensed buyers or refineries with assay certificates instead of informal traders: moving from 88% to 97% of spot adds about USD 129,000 a year at Stage 2 volumes.
3. **Earn ethical-gold premiums.** Mercury-free, traceable, fairly employed production can qualify for Fairmined or Fairtrade certification and premiums per kilogram.
4. **Re-treat old tailings.** Traditional tailings often still hold 1–3 g/t of gold. Clean, mercury-free stockpiles can be processed at low mining cost; mercury-contaminated tailings need specialist clean-up first.
5. **Increase throughput.** Once recovery is stable, remove bottlenecks (usually the mill or the power supply) to spread fixed costs over more tonnes.
6. **Mine the best ore first, within the plan.** Sequence grade blocks to bring cash forward and repay loans faster, without abandoning lower-grade ore the plant can still process profitably.
7. **Add side revenue.** Toll-process ore for neighbouring licensed miners, sell crushed waste rock as aggregate, and sell surplus solar power to the camp or community.
8. **Control gold security.** Seal concentrates, restrict access to the gold room and reconcile ore tonnes, grade and gold produced daily, so leakage shows up immediately.
9. **Manage price risk.** Sell regularly rather than holding stock, and consider forward sales with your buyer for part of production when prices are high and the loan must be repaid.
10. **Keep the licence.** Community benefit agreements, local hiring and visible reclamation protect the social licence that the whole business depends on.

## Health, safety and key risks

The three challenges named for this project — capital intensity, recovery efficiency and occupational health and safety — each have a direct, affordable control.

| Risk | Impact | Control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capital intensity | Equipment fails in harsh conditions; money runs out before production | Stage 1 first; buy robust, locally serviceable brands; keep critical spares; lease mobile equipment; hold 3 months' working capital |
| Poor recovery | Up to 50% or more of free gold lost to tailings | Metallurgical test work; correct grind size; centrifugal concentrators; weekly tailings assays; operator training |
| Silica dust (silicosis) | Permanent lung disease | Wet drilling and wet milling, enclosed crushers with water sprays, P2/N95 respirators, ventilation, regular health checks |
| Ground collapse | Fatalities underground or in pit walls | Engineered shafts with supports, benched pit walls, daily ground inspections, no undercutting |
| Explosives | Injuries, theft, licence loss | Licensed blaster or contractor only; licensed magazine; strict issue-and-return records |
| Cyanide | Acute poisoning, environmental spills | Licensed supply, locked storage, pH control, trained staff, antidote kits and emergency plan, cyanide destruction before discharge |
| Mercury (legacy) | Neurological harm, contaminated land | Zero mercury on site; buy-back and safe disposal of existing stocks; test and isolate old tailings |
| Grade below plan | Profit falls sharply | Measured resource before major investment; regular reconciliation |
| Gold price fall | Lower revenue | Low-cost operation; partial forward sales; keep cash reserves |
| Theft and leakage | 5–15% hidden losses | Sealed concentrates, gold room controls, daily reconciliation |
| Community conflict | Blockades, licence loss | Community agreements, local jobs, grievance process, visible reclamation |
| Regulatory change | Permit suspension | Full compliance and good regulator relations; monitor legal changes |

**Minimum PPE per worker:** hard hat, safety boots, gloves, eye protection, hearing protection, dust respirator, high-visibility clothing; plus chemical-resistant gear and face shields in the leach and smelting areas. Run a short safety talk every shift and keep an accident and near-miss log.

## Implementation roadmap and KPIs

The plan moves from permits to full modern production in about 18–24 months, with each phase gated by a result rather than a date.

1. **Months 0–6: Prepare.** Form the company or cooperative, secure the licence, EIA and community agreements, sample the deposit and run metallurgical tests. *Gate: measured grade and recovery support Stage 1 economics.*
2. **Months 6–9: Build Stage 1.** Install crusher, ball mill, centrifugal concentrator, shaking table, borax furnace, settling ponds; train the crew; remove all mercury from site. *Gate: plant runs at design throughput.*
3. **Months 9–15: Operate and prove.** Run Stage 1, reconcile production daily, build cash reserves, start selling to licensed buyers, stockpile clean tailings. *Gate: six months of stable production and tailings assays confirming leachable gold.*
4. **Months 15–24: Build Stage 2.** Add leach plant, lined TSF, solar hybrid power, water recirculation and assay lab; obtain cyanide permits. *Gate: compliance inspection passed.*
5. **Month 24 onward: Optimise.** Pursue certification, re-treat stockpiled tailings, add toll processing, and reclaim mined-out areas continuously.
